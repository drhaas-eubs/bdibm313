Pre-Reading: Nine Boxes Beat Forty Pages

Key framework: Business Model Canvas

Digital Entrepreneurial Processes · Format: Mini-Hackathon

Learning Focus · ILOs 1 & 2

Investigate how AI and no-code tools change the way a digital venture moves from idea to tested business model, and critically assess the technical and market conditions that make a fast launch possible.

Key framework: Business Model Canvas

The Business Model Canvas is a one-page map of how a venture creates, delivers and captures value, split into nine building blocks. Instead of writing a long business plan once, founders sketch the canvas, mark each block as a hypothesis and update it as tests come back.

Key Points

  • A business model explains how a venture creates, delivers and captures value; the canvas makes it visible on one page.
  • The nine blocks run from customers and value proposition on the right to resources, partners and costs on the left.
  • Every sticky note on a new canvas is an assumption until a customer test supports it.
  • AI and no-code tools make MVPs cheap, so the canvas, not the code, becomes the founder's main planning tool.

Read Before Class

Ask ten founders what a business model is and you get ten answers. Osterwalder and Pigneur (2010) offer a simple one: a business model describes how an organisation creates, delivers and captures value. Their Business Model Canvas splits that idea into nine building blocks: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partners and cost structure. The right side of the canvas is about customers and value; the left side is about the operations needed to deliver it.

Why not simply write a business plan? Traditional plans assume the founder can predict customers, prices and costs for five years. For a new digital venture this is mostly guesswork. Blank (2013) argues that start-ups should search for a business model rather than execute a plan, and Ries (2011) describes the Build–Measure–Learn loop as the engine of that search. The canvas fits this logic: it is quick to draw, easy to change and shows a team exactly which assumptions they still need to test.

AI and no-code tools have pushed this further. Low-code and no-code platforms let people build applications with visual, high-level tools instead of writing code line by line (Bock and Frank, 2021). AI app builders now generate a working prototype from a written description. When an MVP can be built in an afternoon, the bottleneck shifts from building to deciding: which customer segment, which value proposition, which revenue stream? The canvas is where those decisions are made and recorded.

Use the canvas as a test board, not a poster. Start with customer segments and value proposition, then add the other blocks. Colour-code each note: tested or untested. After every experiment, move or replace notes, and keep old versions so you can see how the model evolved. Bland and Osterwalder (2019) recommend starting with the riskiest assumption, the one that would kill the business if wrong. In class you will do exactly that with your own MVP.
