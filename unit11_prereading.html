Pre-Reading: Ten Stories Are Not Yet a Lesson

Key framework: Cross-Case Synthesis

Key Case Findings and Roundtable Discussion · Format: Roundtable · Capstone

Learning Focus · Integrates ILOs 1–4

Integrate the course by comparing ten real digital ventures, critically assessing the technical and social conditions behind their outcomes, reflecting on your own entrepreneurial leadership, and communicating how digitally governed businesses differ from traditional ones.

Key framework: Cross-Case Synthesis

Cross-case synthesis compares several cases using the same set of questions so that patterns, differences and explanations become visible. Instead of asking ‘what happened at Gorillas?’, you ask ‘what happens to fast-growing digital ventures, and why?’

Key Points

  • Use the same questions (columns) for every case, or you will compare apples with pears.
  • A pattern counts only when it appears in several cases — and you can explain why.
  • Look for ‘polar types’: put a clear success next to a clear failure and ask what differs.
  • Always search for the case that breaks your pattern; it makes your argument stronger.

Read Before Class

Over this course you have met ten ventures, from Gorillas in Berlin to Sama’s data-labelling teams in Nairobi. Each case was useful on its own, but a single case can mislead: it is easy to blame one founder, one market or one bad year. Cross-case synthesis is the method researchers use to move from stories to lessons. Eisenhardt (1989) showed that comparing several cases systematically lets you build explanations that are more reliable than anything a single case can offer.

The technique is simple. First, decide on a small set of questions you will ask of every case, for example the digital lever, the enabling conditions, the main risk, how the founders learned, and the outcome. Yin (2018) recommends placing the answers in a word table, with cases as rows and questions as columns. Once the table is filled, you read down the columns rather than across the rows, looking for answers that repeat.

The second step is to compare cases that ended differently. Eisenhardt and Graebner (2007) suggest choosing ‘polar types’ — a clear success and a clear failure — because the contrast makes the cause easier to see. Vinted and Gorillas both built consumer platforms in Europe, yet one reached profit after patient, data-driven growth while the other was sold at a fraction of its peak value. Asking ‘what is different?’ is often more revealing than asking ‘what went wrong?’.

Finally, treat every pattern as a claim that needs evidence. A good pattern is supported by at least three cases, comes with a reason why it should hold, and survives the search for counter-examples (Eisenhardt, 1989). In today’s roundtable you will defend your pattern in public, so bring the data. That discipline — evidence first, conclusion second — is exactly what investors, juries and your final project markers will expect.
