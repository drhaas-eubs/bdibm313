Pre-Reading: Your Runway Is Your Deadline

Key framework: Burn Rate, Runway & Fume Date

Financing Well-informed Digital Entrepreneurial Ventures · Format: Pitch Simulation

Learning Focus · ILOs 3 & 4

Evaluate how founders reflect on and lead fundraising decisions, and communicate how digital ventures are financed differently from traditional businesses, using burn rate, runway and investor criteria.

Key framework: Burn Rate, Runway & Fume Date

Burn rate tells you how fast cash leaves the company, runway tells you how many months you have left, and the fume date tells you the day the money runs out. Together they decide when you must raise money and how strong your negotiating position will be.

Key Points

  • Net monthly burn = monthly cash out − monthly cash in.
  • Runway (months) = cash in the bank ÷ net monthly burn.
  • Start fundraising at least 6–9 months before the fume date, because rounds take time to clear.
  • Debt, grants and bootstrapping can extend runway without giving away shares.

Read Before Class

Most start-ups do not die because their idea is bad. They die because they run out of cash before the idea can prove itself. That is why investors ask three questions in almost every pitch: how much are you burning, how long is your runway, and when will you need more money? Burn rate is the cash you spend each month; net burn subtracts the cash that customers pay you. Runway is simply cash divided by net burn. The fume date is the calendar day on which, if nothing changes, the account reaches zero (Feld and Mendelson, 2019).

Paul Graham turned these numbers into a sharper test. A start-up is 'default alive' if, with its current growth and costs, it reaches profitability before the money runs out; it is 'default dead' if it depends on the next round (Graham, 2015). This matters because raising money takes time — often six months or more from first meetings to cash in the bank. If you start too late, you negotiate from weakness and accept worse terms or no deal at all.

Which money should fill the gap? Research shows that the right source depends on a venture's stage. Very young firms rely on founders, friends and business angels; venture capital and bank debt become available only when there is evidence such as revenue or data (Berger and Udell, 1998). In Germany, public programmes such as the KfW ERP-Gründerkredit StartGeld, which offers founders loans of up to €125,000 through their own bank with KfW covering 80% of the bank's risk, can help at the start (KfW, 2025). Venture debt can extend runway later, but it must be repaid and often comes with strict conditions.

The Builder.ai case shows what happens when the numbers do not hold. After an audit reportedly found 2024 revenue far below the figures given to lenders, its main lender seized most of its cash and the company entered insolvency within days (Sun, 2025a; Sun, 2025b). The lesson for you is practical: keep your burn sheet honest, update it monthly, and never let a good story replace verified revenue.
