Pre-Reading: Legal Is Not the Same as Fair

Key framework: Carroll's CSR Pyramid

Ethical Models and Practices Affecting Digitalization · Format: Ethics Tribunal

Learning Focus · ILOs 2, 3 & 4

Critically assess the technical and social conditions behind ethical problems in digital ventures, reflect on one's own ethical stance under pressure, and communicate how ethics shifts from traditional to digital business operations.

Key framework: Carroll's CSR Pyramid

Carroll's pyramid shows four layers of business responsibility: make a profit, obey the law, act ethically beyond the law and give back to society. Digital ventures often meet the first two layers but are tested on the third, where no law tells them exactly what to do.

Key Points

  • Economic and legal responsibilities are the foundation, but not the whole building.
  • Ethical responsibilities begin where the law ends — especially across borders.
  • Outsourcing moves work, not responsibility.
  • Digital scale makes small ethical gaps very large very quickly.

Read Before Class

When a bookshop sold books in 1990, ethics meant fair prices, honest advertising and decent pay for the staff you could see. Digital ventures work differently. Their products depend on data, algorithms and people who may sit in another country and work for another company. Archie Carroll's pyramid of corporate social responsibility helps sort the questions this raises. At the bottom are economic responsibilities: a business must be profitable to survive. Above that are legal responsibilities: it must obey the law. Then come ethical responsibilities — doing what is right and fair even when no law requires it — and at the top, philanthropic contributions (Carroll, 1991).

The difficult layer for digital ventures is the ethical one. Laws differ between countries and change slowly, while digital business models scale across borders in months. The European Union has responded with rules such as the GDPR for personal data and the AI Act for risky AI systems (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2016; 2024). But neither law tells a start-up what fair pay or adequate psychological support for a data labeller in Nairobi looks like. That remains an ethical judgement.

The Sama and OpenAI case illustrates this. TIME reported that Kenyan workers labelling harmful text for ChatGPT took home less than $2 per hour, while Sama said it offered mental health support and fair wages and OpenAI called the work valuable for AI safety (Perrigo, 2023). Researchers call this hidden labour 'ghost work' (Gray and Suri, 2019). The Fairwork project proposes concrete tests — fair pay, conditions, contracts, management and representation — to make such work visible and comparable (Graham et al., 2020).

For you as a future founder, the lesson is practical. Ask before launch: who does the work my product needs, who carries its risks, and would I defend these conditions in public? Using Carroll's pyramid as a checklist turns ethics from a vague feeling into a design decision you can explain to customers, investors and regulators. In the tribunal, you will test exactly this: can your arguments survive questions from workers, clients and regulators at the same time?
