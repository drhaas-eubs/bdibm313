SLIBRARY 1 — DIGITAL ENTREPRENEURSHIP FOUNDATIONS

Nambisan's Digital Entrepreneurship Elements

A conceptual lens that explains digital entrepreneurship through three distinct but related elements: digital artifacts (software-based components, applications and content), digital platforms (shared sets of services and architecture that host complementary offerings) and digital infrastructure (digital tools and systems that support collaboration, communication and innovation). It shows that digital technologies make entrepreneurial processes and outcomes less bounded and the locus of entrepreneurial agency less predefined (Nambisan, 2017).

Artifacts, platforms, infrastructure: the three building blocks of digital ventures.

EXAMPLESpotify’s app and recommendation algorithm are digital artifacts, its marketplace for artists and podcasters is a platform, and it runs on cloud services and mobile networks as infrastructure.
REFERENCE

Nambisan, S. (2017) 'Digital entrepreneurship: toward a digital technology perspective of entrepreneurship', Entrepreneurship Theory and Practice, 41(6), pp. 1029–1055.

SLIBRARY 1 — DIGITAL ENTREPRENEURSHIP FOUNDATIONS

Degrees of Digitization

A typology that classifies new digital ventures as mild, moderate or extreme according to how far their products, services, distribution and workplace are digital. The degree of digitization shapes the success factors of a venture, including its reliance on computer-mediated communication and virtual workplaces (Hull et al., 2007).

Mild, moderate or extreme: how much of the business is digital?

EXAMPLEA Munich bakery with online pre-orders is mildly digital, Too Good To Go is moderately digital because the food is physical, and a language app such as Babbel is extremely digital.
REFERENCE

Hull, C.E., Hung, Y.-T.C., Hair, N., Perotti, V. and DeMartino, R. (2007) 'Taking advantage of digital opportunities: a typology of digital entrepreneurship', International Journal of Networking and Virtual Organisations, 4(3), pp. 290–303.

SLIBRARY 1 — DIGITAL ENTREPRENEURSHIP FOUNDATIONS

Timmons Model of the Entrepreneurial Process

A model that describes entrepreneurship as a dynamic balancing act between three driving forces: the opportunity, the resources and the team. The lead entrepreneur keeps these forces in fit under conditions of ambiguity, uncertainty and changing external forces, with the opportunity as the starting point (Spinelli and Adams, 2016).

Balance opportunity, resources and team, led by the founder.

EXAMPLEGorillas had a large team and huge resources in 2021, but the opportunity (profitable ten-minute delivery) proved smaller than assumed, so the model tipped out of balance.
REFERENCE

Timmons, J.A. and Spinelli, S. (2009) New Venture Creation: Entrepreneurship for the 21st Century. 8th edn. Boston, MA: McGraw-Hill/Irwin.

Digital Entrepreneurship Ecosystem

A framework that integrates the digital ecosystem and the entrepreneurial ecosystem to explain how agents and users interact in the digital age. It consists of four concepts: digital infrastructure governance, digital user citizenship, digital entrepreneurship and the digital marketplace, connected through multisided platforms (Sussan and Acs, 2017).

Infrastructure, users, entrepreneurs and markets interact around platforms.

EXAMPLEA Munich app developer depends on Apple’s App Store rules (infrastructure governance), on user trust and data rights under the GDPR (user citizenship) and on the store as a marketplace.
REFERENCE

Sussan, F. and Acs, Z.J. (2017) 'The digital entrepreneurial ecosystem', Small Business Economics, 49(1), pp. 55–73.

SLIBRARY 2 — TRADITIONAL VS DIGITAL BUSINESS

Rogers' Five Domains of Digital Transformation

A strategic framework stating that digital technologies change the rules of business in five domains: customers, competition, data, innovation and value. Firms transform successfully when they rethink their strategy in all five domains rather than simply upgrading their technology (Rogers, 2016).

Customers, competition, data, innovation, value: rethink all five.

EXAMPLEVinted treats its customers as a network of sellers and buyers, uses data to price and match items and earns value from buyer fees rather than from owning stock.
REFERENCE

Rogers, D.L. (2016) The Digital Transformation Playbook: Rethink Your Business for the Digital Age. New York: Columbia Business School Publishing.

SLIBRARY 2 — TRADITIONAL VS DIGITAL BUSINESS

Disruptive Innovation

Disruptive innovation describes a process whereby a smaller company with fewer resources successfully challenges established incumbents, typically by targeting overlooked low-end or new-market customers with simpler, cheaper offerings and then moving upmarket. Incumbents often ignore the entrant because they focus on improving products for their most profitable customers (Christensen, Raynor and McDonald, 2015).

Small entrants start at the bottom and move upmarket.

EXAMPLEN26 started with a simple mobile account for digitally native young customers whom traditional banks found unattractive, then added premium accounts and services.
REFERENCE

Christensen, C.M. (1997) The Innovator’s Dilemma: When New Technologies Cause Great Firms to Fail. Boston, MA: Harvard Business School Press.

SLIBRARY 2 — TRADITIONAL VS DIGITAL BUSINESS

Westerman Digital Maturity Matrix

A two-by-two matrix that classifies organisations by their digital capabilities (what technology-enabled initiatives they invest in) and their leadership capabilities (how they envision, govern and lead transformation). It distinguishes Beginners, Fashionistas, Conservatives and Digital Masters, the last of which combine both capabilities and tend to outperform their peers financially (Westerman, Bonnet and McAfee, 2014).

Digital tools plus leadership make a digital master.

EXAMPLEA Munich fashion retailer that launches an app, TikTok shop and AI chatbot without a clear vision or governance is a Fashionista; Zalando, which ties its platform strategy to leadership and data, is closer to a Digital Master.
REFERENCE

Westerman, G., Bonnet, D. and McAfee, A. (2014) Leading Digital: Turning Technology into Business Transformation. Boston, MA: Harvard Business Review Press.

SLIBRARY 2 — TRADITIONAL VS DIGITAL BUSINESS

Pipelines vs Platforms

A strategic distinction between pipeline businesses, which create value by controlling a linear series of activities from inputs to customers, and platform businesses, which create value by facilitating interactions between external producers and consumers. Platforms shift strategy from controlling resources to orchestrating them, from optimising internal processes to facilitating external interactions and from customer value to ecosystem value (Van Alstyne, Parker and Choudary, 2016).

Pipelines push products down a chain; platforms connect producers and consumers.

EXAMPLEGorillas owned its stock, stores and riders like a pipeline, whereas Too Good To Go connects existing bakeries and restaurants with consumers without owning the food.
REFERENCE

Van Alstyne, M.W., Parker, G.G. and Choudary, S.P. (2016) 'Pipelines, platforms, and the new rules of strategy', Harvard Business Review, 94(4), pp. 54–60, 62.

SLIBRARY 3 — TECHNOLOGY WAVES & DIFFUSION

Techno-Economic Paradigms

A theory of long waves stating that capitalism has passed through successive technological revolutions, each creating a new techno-economic paradigm, a set of best practices that reshapes the whole economy. Each surge passes through an installation period driven by financial capital and speculative frenzy, a turning point often marked by a crash, and a deployment period in which production capital and institutions spread the benefits widely (Perez, 2002).

Each technological revolution: installation frenzy, turning point, then deployment.

EXAMPLEThe quick-commerce boom of 2021 and the generative AI funding surge after 2023 both show installation-period behaviour, with huge capital flows into young start-ups before business models are proven.
REFERENCE

Perez, C. (2002) Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital: The Dynamics of Bubbles and Golden Ages. Cheltenham: Edward Elgar.

SLIBRARY 3 — TECHNOLOGY WAVES & DIFFUSION

Diffusion of Innovations

Diffusion is the process in which an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time among the members of a social system. Adopters fall into five categories, innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority and laggards, and the speed of adoption depends on perceived attributes such as relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability and observability (Rogers, 2003).

New ideas spread from innovators to laggards through social systems.

EXAMPLEMistral 7B was first adopted by AI developers and researchers (innovators) who downloaded the open model, which helped convince companies (early majority) to test it later.
REFERENCE

Rogers, E.M. (2003) Diffusion of Innovations. 5th edn. New York: Free Press.

SLIBRARY 3 — TECHNOLOGY WAVES & DIFFUSION

Gartner Hype Cycle

A graphical model of how expectations about an emerging technology evolve over time, through five phases: innovation trigger, peak of inflated expectations, trough of disillusionment, slope of enlightenment and plateau of productivity. It helps organisations separate hype from real business value when deciding when to adopt a technology (Fenn and Raskino, 2008).

Expectations rise, crash, then recover into real productivity.

EXAMPLEQuick-commerce apps like Gorillas rode a peak of inflated expectations in 2021 and fell into the trough in 2022, while generative AI start-ups faced a similar test of expectations after 2023.
REFERENCE

Fenn, J. and Raskino, M. (2008) Mastering the Hype Cycle: How to Choose the Right Innovation at the Right Time. Boston, MA: Harvard Business Press.

SLIBRARY 3 — TECHNOLOGY WAVES & DIFFUSION

The Second Machine Age forces

An explanation of the current digital revolution as a second machine age, in which computers do for mental power what the steam engine did for muscle power. Three forces drive it: exponential improvement in computing, the digitisation of almost everything and combinatorial (recombinant) innovation built on existing digital components (Brynjolfsson and McAfee, 2014).

Exponential, digital and combinatorial: three forces multiplying mental power.

EXAMPLEMistral combined cheaper and faster GPUs (exponential), vast digitised text (digital) and openly published AI techniques (combinatorial) to build competitive models with a small team.
REFERENCE

Brynjolfsson, E. and McAfee, A. (2014) The Second Machine Age: Work, Progress, and Prosperity in a Time of Brilliant Technologies. New York: W.W. Norton.

SLIBRARY 4 — PLATFORMS, ACTORS & SOCIO-TECHNICAL CHANGE

Platform Ecosystem / Core Interaction

A platform design framework stating that every platform is built around a core interaction, in which participants (producers and consumers) exchange a value unit selected by a filter. The platform ecosystem includes four roles, owners, providers, producers and consumers, and the platform’s job is to attract, facilitate and match them (Parker, Van Alstyne and Choudary, 2016).

Design one core interaction: participants exchange a value unit through a filter.

EXAMPLEOn Hugging Face, AI developers (producers) share models (value units) that other developers and companies (consumers) find through search and rankings (the filter); Mistral used such channels to spread its open models.
REFERENCE

Parker, G.G., Van Alstyne, M.W. and Choudary, S.P. (2016) Platform Revolution: How Networked Markets Are Transforming the Economy and How to Make Them Work for You. New York: W.W. Norton.

SLIBRARY 4 — PLATFORMS, ACTORS & SOCIO-TECHNICAL CHANGE

Network Effects

Network effects (network externalities) exist when the value a user gains from a product increases with the number of other users of that product or of compatible products. They create positive feedback, so markets with strong network effects tend to tip towards one or a few dominant players (Katz and Shapiro, 1985; Shapiro and Varian, 1999).

Each new user makes the product more valuable for everyone.

EXAMPLEThe more developers build tools and fine-tuned versions of Mistral’s open models, the more attractive those models become for the next developer and company.
REFERENCE

Katz, M.L. and Shapiro, C. (1985) 'Network externalities, competition, and compatibility', American Economic Review, 75(3), pp. 424–440.

SLIBRARY 4 — PLATFORMS, ACTORS & SOCIO-TECHNICAL CHANGE

Multi-Level Perspective on socio-technical transitions

A framework that explains technological transitions as changes in socio-technical configurations resulting from the interplay of three levels: niches, where radical innovations emerge; socio-technical regimes, the stable rules, technologies, practices and actors of a sector; and the socio-technical landscape of broad external trends. Transitions occur when niche innovations build momentum while landscape pressures destabilise the regime (Geels, 2002).

Niches challenge regimes when landscape pressure opens a window.

EXAMPLEOpen-weight AI models (niche) challenge the closed-model regime of US technology giants, helped by landscape pressure such as Europe’s push for digital sovereignty.
REFERENCE

Geels, F.W. (2002) 'Technological transitions as evolutionary reconfiguration processes: a multi-level perspective and a case-study', Research Policy, 31(8–9), pp. 1257–1274.

SLIBRARY 4 — PLATFORMS, ACTORS & SOCIO-TECHNICAL CHANGE

Startup Life Cycle stages

A stage model of high-growth technology start-ups based on data from thousands of companies, describing a sequence of Discovery, Validation, Efficiency and Scale, followed by later stages of Sustain and Conservation. Start-ups that advance one dimension, such as hiring or spending, out of sync with the others suffer premature scaling, the most common cause of failure in the study (Marmer et al., 2011).

Discovery, Validation, Efficiency, Scale: do not scale too early.

EXAMPLEGorillas expanded into many countries before proving efficient unit economics, a textbook case of premature scaling; Mistral raised large sums but tied them to compute and products customers were already buying.
REFERENCE

Marmer, M., Herrmann, B.L., Dogrultan, E. and Berman, R. (2011) Startup Genome Report: A New Framework for Understanding Why Startups Succeed. Startup Genome.

SLIBRARY 5 — FROM IDEA TO VENTURE

Lean Startup Build–Measure–Learn

The Build–Measure–Learn loop is the core feedback cycle of the Lean Startup method, in which a venture turns ideas into a minimum viable product, measures how customers respond and uses the data to decide whether to pivot or persevere. Progress is measured in validated learning rather than in features delivered (Ries, 2011).

Build small, measure real behaviour, learn, then pivot or persevere.

EXAMPLELovable users can generate a working app in hours, test it with ten target users and change the product the next day, running several Build–Measure–Learn loops in a single week.
REFERENCE

Ries, E. (2011) The Lean Startup: How Today's Entrepreneurs Use Continuous Innovation to Create Radically Successful Businesses. New York: Crown Business.

SLIBRARY 5 — FROM IDEA TO VENTURE

Customer Development

Customer Development is a four-step process for start-ups (customer discovery, customer validation, customer creation and company building) that runs alongside product development and treats the business model as a set of hypotheses to be tested directly with customers. A start-up is seen as a temporary organisation searching for a repeatable and scalable business model (Blank and Dorf, 2012).

Get out of the building: customers hold the facts.

EXAMPLEBefore building a student flat-sharing app, a team interviews 20 international students in Munich and discovers that the real pain is deposit fraud, not finding listings, and changes its value proposition.
REFERENCE

Blank, S. and Dorf, B. (2012) The Startup Owner's Manual: The Step-by-Step Guide for Building a Great Company. Pescadero, CA: K&S Ranch.

SLIBRARY 5 — FROM IDEA TO VENTURE

Effectuation

Effectuation is a logic of entrepreneurial decision-making in which founders start from the means they already control (who they are, what they know and whom they know) and co-create possible outcomes with partners, rather than first setting a goal and then predicting the best path to it (causation). It focuses on what can be controlled instead of what can be predicted (Sarasvathy, 2001).

Start with what you have, not with a perfect forecast.

EXAMPLEA student who speaks three languages and knows local restaurant owners starts a menu-translation service for them, using a free AI tool and an affordable loss of 200 euros, instead of writing a full plan first.
REFERENCE

Sarasvathy, S.D. (2001) 'Causation and effectuation: toward a theoretical shift from economic inevitability to entrepreneurial contingency', Academy of Management Review, 26(2), pp. 243–263.

SLIBRARY 5 — FROM IDEA TO VENTURE

PESTEL analysis

PESTEL analysis is a structured scan of the macro-environment of an organisation across political, economic, social, technological, environmental and legal factors. It grew out of Aguilar's work on how managers scan their business environment for relevant external information (Aguilar, 1967).

Scan six outside forces before they scan you.

EXAMPLEA Munich food-delivery start-up checks the EU platform-work rules (legal), inflation (economic), demand for sustainable packaging (environmental) and AI route planning (technological) before choosing its launch city.
REFERENCE

Aguilar, F.J. (1967) Scanning the Business Environment. New York: Macmillan.

Business Model Canvas

The Business Model Canvas is a visual template that describes a business model through nine building blocks: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships and cost structure. It shows how an organisation creates, delivers and captures value (Osterwalder and Pigneur, 2010).

Nine boxes on one page show how a venture makes money.

EXAMPLEVinted's canvas shows free listing for sellers as a value proposition, a buyer protection fee as the revenue stream and its own parcel network as a key resource.
REFERENCE

Osterwalder, A. and Pigneur, Y. (2010) Business Model Generation: A Handbook for Visionaries, Game Changers, and Challengers. Hoboken, NJ: John Wiley & Sons.

SLIBRARY 6 — BUSINESS MODEL DESIGN & MVP

Value Proposition Canvas

The Value Proposition Canvas is a tool that zooms into two blocks of the Business Model Canvas: a customer profile (jobs, pains and gains) and a value map (products and services, pain relievers and gain creators). Fit is achieved when the value map addresses the most important jobs, pains and gains of the customer (Osterwalder et al., 2014).

Match what you offer to the jobs, pains and gains customers have.

EXAMPLEFor students selling clothes on Vinted, the job is 'clear my wardrobe and earn money', the pain is fees and effort, and free listing plus prepaid labels act as pain relievers.
REFERENCE

Osterwalder, A., Pigneur, Y., Bernarda, G. and Smith, A. (2014) Value Proposition Design: How to Create Products and Services Customers Want. Hoboken, NJ: John Wiley & Sons.

SLIBRARY 6 — BUSINESS MODEL DESIGN & MVP

Lean Canvas

The Lean Canvas is an adaptation of the Business Model Canvas for start-ups that replaces four blocks with problem, solution, key metrics and unfair advantage, keeping the focus on the riskiest assumptions of a new venture. It is designed to be completed quickly and updated as the founder learns (Maurya, 2012).

A start-up's business model, starting from the problem.

EXAMPLEA hackathon team writes 'Erasmus students cannot find a short-term flat before arrival' as the problem and 'number of verified bookings per week' as the key metric.
REFERENCE

Maurya, A. (2012) Running Lean: Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works. 2nd edn. Sebastopol, CA: O'Reilly Media.

SLIBRARY 6 — BUSINESS MODEL DESIGN & MVP

Testing Business Ideas — Desirability, Feasibility, Viability

Testing Business Ideas is an experimentation approach that sorts business hypotheses into three risk types (desirability, feasibility and viability) and matches each with fast, cheap experiments, starting with the riskiest assumption. The aim is to reduce the risk of a new idea step by step before investing heavily (Bland and Osterwalder, 2019).

Do they want it, can we build it, should we?

EXAMPLEA team building an AI study-planner tests desirability with a fake-door landing page, feasibility by checking that user data stays protected and viability by asking students to pre-pay 3 euros.
REFERENCE

Bland, D.J. and Osterwalder, A. (2019) Testing Business Ideas: A Field Guide for Rapid Experimentation. Hoboken, NJ: John Wiley & Sons.

SLIBRARY 7 — OPPORTUNITY, RISK & STRATEGY

SWOT/TOWS

The TOWS matrix extends SWOT analysis by systematically combining external opportunities and threats with internal strengths and weaknesses to generate four types of strategy: SO (maxi-maxi), ST (maxi-mini), WO (mini-maxi) and WT (mini-mini). It turns a descriptive list into strategic options (Weihrich, 1982).

Match strengths and weaknesses to opportunities and threats to find moves.

EXAMPLEA Munich ride-sharing start-up combines its strength (strong student community) with an opportunity (rising public transport prices) into an SO strategy: campus-to-campus shared rides.
REFERENCE

Weihrich, H. (1982) 'The TOWS matrix: a tool for situational analysis', Long Range Planning, 15(2), pp. 54–66.

SLIBRARY 7 — OPPORTUNITY, RISK & STRATEGY

Porter's Five Forces

Porter's Five Forces is a framework for analysing industry structure through five competitive forces: rivalry among existing competitors, the threat of new entrants, the bargaining power of buyers, the bargaining power of suppliers and the threat of substitutes. Together these forces determine the profit potential of an industry (Porter, 2008).

Five pressures decide how much profit an industry leaves you.

EXAMPLEFor second-hand fashion apps, buyer power is high because switching between apps is easy, which explains why Vinted invests in trust and convenience to keep users.
REFERENCE

Porter, M.E. (1979) 'How competitive forces shape strategy', Harvard Business Review, 57(2), pp. 137–145.

SLIBRARY 7 — OPPORTUNITY, RISK & STRATEGY

Ansoff Matrix

The Ansoff Matrix, or product–market growth matrix, sets out four growth strategies by combining existing or new products with existing or new markets: market penetration, market development, product development and diversification. Risk rises as a firm moves away from its current products and markets (Ansoff, 1957).

Four ways to grow: same or new products, same or new markets.

EXAMPLEVinted used market development by launching in new European countries and product development by adding categories such as electronics (Vinted, 2025).
REFERENCE

Ansoff, H.I. (1957) 'Strategies for diversification', Harvard Business Review, 35(5), pp. 113–124.

SLIBRARY 7 — OPPORTUNITY, RISK & STRATEGY

Blue Ocean ERRC Grid

The ERRC Grid is a tool from Blue Ocean Strategy that asks which industry factors should be eliminated, reduced, raised and created to deliver a leap in value for buyers at lower cost. It supports value innovation, the pursuit of differentiation and low cost at the same time (Kim and Mauborgne, 2005).

Eliminate, reduce, raise, create: redesign value, escape the red ocean.

EXAMPLEVinted eliminated seller fees, reduced listing effort with a simple app, raised trust with buyer protection and created its own pick-up network with Vinted Go.
REFERENCE

Kim, W.C. and Mauborgne, R. (2005) Blue Ocean Strategy: How to Create Uncontested Market Space and Make the Competition Irrelevant. Boston, MA: Harvard Business School Press.

SLIBRARY 8 — RESOURCES, CHANNELS & BUDGET

VRIO / Resource-Based View

The resource-based view argues that sustained competitive advantage comes from firm resources that are valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable and non-substitutable. The VRIO framework turns this into four questions about value, rarity, imitability and organisation (Barney, 1991; 1995).

Valuable, rare, hard to copy and organised: real advantage.

EXAMPLEA recommendation algorithm may be valuable but easy to copy, while Vinted's network of pick-up points and its user base are rare and costly to imitate.
REFERENCE

Barney, J.B. (1991) 'Firm resources and sustained competitive advantage', Journal of Management, 17(1), pp. 99–120.

SLIBRARY 8 — RESOURCES, CHANNELS & BUDGET

Bullseye Traction Framework

The Bullseye Framework is a three-step process for finding the customer acquisition channel that will bring a start-up its next stage of growth: brainstorm ideas for all 19 traction channels, run cheap tests on the most promising, and focus resources on the channel that works best. It treats traction as a discipline equal to product development (Weinberg and Mares, 2015).

Brainstorm 19 channels, test three, focus on one.

EXAMPLEA study-notes app tests TikTok content, university society partnerships and SEO; partnerships bring users at a quarter of the cost, so the team focuses there.
REFERENCE

Weinberg, G. and Mares, J. (2015) Traction: How Any Startup Can Achieve Explosive Customer Growth. New York: Portfolio/Penguin.

SLIBRARY 8 — RESOURCES, CHANNELS & BUDGET

Paid–Owned–Earned Media (POEM)

Paid–Owned–Earned Media is a framework that classifies marketing channels by control and cost: paid media are bought (ads, sponsorships), owned media are controlled by the brand (website, app, newsletter, social accounts) and earned media are generated by others (reviews, word of mouth, press). Effective cross-media strategies combine all three (Corcoran, 2009).

Buy attention, own the channel, earn the conversation.

EXAMPLEVinted combines paid TV ads, its owned app and newsletter, and earned media from users sharing their finds and from branded series coverage (Gollasch, 2025).
REFERENCE

Corcoran, S. (2009) 'Defining earned, owned and paid media', Forrester Blogs, 16 December. Available at: https://www.forrester.com/blogs/09-12-16-defining_earned_owned_and_paid_media/ (Accessed: 4 October 2026).

SLIBRARY 8 — RESOURCES, CHANNELS & BUDGET

Unit Economics: LTV/CAC

Unit economics compares the lifetime value of a customer, the present value of the margins a customer generates over the relationship, with the cost of acquiring that customer. A venture grows profitably only when lifetime value clearly exceeds acquisition cost (Gupta, Lehmann and Stuart, 2004).

A customer must be worth more than it costs to win.

EXAMPLEIf a marketplace pays €12 to acquire a buyer who generates €3 of fees per year and stays four years, the LTV of about €12 only just covers the cost.
REFERENCE

Gupta, S., Lehmann, D.R. and Stuart, J.A. (2004) 'Valuing customers', Journal of Marketing Research, 41(1), pp. 7–18.

SLIBRARY 19 — ETHICAL LENSES

Three Ethical Theories

Normative ethical theories offer different tests for right action: consequentialism judges actions by their outcomes for everyone affected, deontology by whether they respect duties and rights regardless of outcomes, and virtue ethics by whether they reflect the character of a good person or organisation. Business ethics uses these lenses together to reach more balanced decisions (Crane et al., 2019).

Judge an action by its outcomes, its duties and its character.

EXAMPLEOutsourcing data labelling to Nairobi may lower costs and create jobs (outcomes), but if workers lack adequate psychological support, duties of care are at stake (duties).
REFERENCE

Crane, A., Matten, D., Glozer, S. and Spence, L.J. (2019) Business Ethics: Managing Corporate Citizenship and Sustainability in the Age of Globalization. 5th edn. Oxford: Oxford University Press.

Carroll's CSR Pyramid

Carroll's pyramid divides corporate social responsibility into four layers: economic responsibilities (be profitable), legal (obey the law), ethical (do what is right, fair and just beyond the law) and philanthropic (be a good corporate citizen). Be profitable, obey the law, be ethical, then give back.

Carroll, A.B. (1991) 'The pyramid of corporate social responsibility: toward the moral management of organizational stakeholders', Business Horizons, 34(4), pp. 39–48.

Value sensitive design is a theoretically grounded approach to designing technology that accounts for human values, such as privacy, autonomy and fairness, throughout the design process. Floridi and Cowls compared major AI ethics guidelines and found they converge on five principles: beneficence (promote well-being), non-maleficence (avoid harm, including to privacy and security), autonomy (preserve human decision-making), justice (promote fairness and avoid discrimination) and explicability (intelligibility and accountability) (Floridi and Cowls, 2019).

EU AI Act Risk Pyramid

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act regulates AI systems according to risk: practices with unacceptable risk are prohibited, high-risk systems must meet requirements such as risk management, data governance and human oversight, limited-risk systems carry transparency duties, and minimal-risk systems are largely unregulated (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024).

Cross-Case Synthesis

Cross-case synthesis is an analytic technique that compares two or more cases on a common set of dimensions, usually in a word table, to identify replicated patterns and explain differences. Findings are strengthened when a pattern holds across cases and survives deliberate comparison with contrasting cases (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 2018).

Same questions, many cases: read down the columns for patterns.

EXAMPLEComparing Vinted and Gorillas on unit economics and growth speed shows why a patient marketplace reached profit while a cash-hungry quick-commerce model was sold below its peak valuation.
REFERENCE

Eisenhardt, K.M. (1989) 'Building theories from case study research', Academy of Management Review, 14(4), pp. 532–550.

Pyramid Principle

The Pyramid Principle is a method for structuring business communication in which the main conclusion is stated first and supported by a small number of logically grouped key arguments, each backed by evidence. Ideas at each level summarise those grouped below them (Minto, 2009).

Toulmin Model of Argument

The Toulmin Model breaks a practical argument into six parts: claim, data (grounds), warrant, backing, qualifier and rebuttal. It shows how evidence is connected to a conclusion and how strong that conclusion really is (Toulmin, 1958).

Six Thinking Hats

Six Thinking Hats is a parallel-thinking method in which a group looks at an issue through one mode of thinking at a time — facts (white), feelings (red), caution (black), benefits (yellow), creativity (green) and process (blue). It separates thinking modes to reduce adversarial debate and improve group decisions (de Bono, 1985).

One perspective at a time, everyone wearing the same hat.

EXAMPLEA Munich student team reviewing its delivery-app idea spends five minutes in the black hat listing risks Gorillas faced, then five in the green hat inventing ways to avoid them.

de Bono, E. (1985) Six Thinking Hats. Boston: Little, Brown and Company.

Reflection-in-Action

Reflection-in-action is the ability of professionals to notice surprises and adjust their approach while they are still acting, as opposed to reflection-on-action, which happens afterwards. It describes how practitioners learn through a reflective conversation with the situation (Schön, 1983).

SLIBRARY 22 — REFLECTION & LEADERSHIP LEARNING

Johari Window

The Johari Window is a 2x2 model of interpersonal awareness that classifies information about a person by whether it is known to self and known to others, producing four areas: open, blind, hidden and unknown. Feedback and self-disclosure enlarge the open area (Luft and Ingham, 1955).

Grow the open area: ask for feedback, share more.

EXAMPLEPeer cards tell a founder he interrupts colleagues in meetings (blind area); he shares that he is anxious about the deadline (hidden area) and agrees to let others finish first.
REFERENCE

Luft, J. and Ingham, H. (1955) 'The Johari window, a graphic model of interpersonal awareness', Proceedings of the Western Training Laboratory in Group Development. Los Angeles: University of California, Los Angeles.

After-Action Review

An After-Action Review (AAR) is a structured, non-blaming team discussion after an event that compares what was supposed to happen with what actually happened, explains the difference and agrees what to sustain or improve. It turns team experience into immediate, shared lessons (Garvin, 2000).

Expected, happened, why, next time — in fifteen minutes.

EXAMPLEAfter today’s rehearsal a team notes: intended 5 minutes, took 8; reason, too many market numbers; next time, one chart and the answer first.
REFERENCE

Garvin, D.A. (2000) Learning in Action: A Guide to Putting the Learning Organization to Work. Boston: Harvard Business School Press.
