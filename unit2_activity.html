BDIBM313 · Unit 2 · Role Play

Role Play — The Paris AI Table

Students take on the real actors behind Mistral AI (founders, investors, chipmakers, cloud partners, regulators, developers, customers) and negotiate what a European AI venture needs to survive.

Main Drivers of the Digital Revolutions · Format: Role Play

Learning Focus · ILO 2

Critically assess the technical and social conditions, such as talent, compute, platforms, consumer habits and policy, that enable a digital revolution and allow digital ventures to emerge and grow.

Technology does not start revolutions; people who combine new tools with new habits do.

Warm-up · 10 min · My First Time

Each student writes on a sticky note the year they first used a smartphone, a streaming service and an AI chatbot. Students line up along the wall in order of when they first used an AI chatbot and compare with the smartphone line. The lecturer asks the first and last person in each line what made them start: a friend, school, work or curiosity.

The Provocation

The steam engine did not change the world in a day. Neither did ChatGPT.

Mistral AI raised more than 100 million euros four weeks after it was founded, before it had a product.

Talent, chips, cloud, open code, money and rules: no single founder owns all of them.

A revolution starts when millions of people change a habit, not when an engineer finishes a model.

Today you are not students. You are the people who decide whether Europe gets its own AI champion.

Read the lines while showing a timeline from the steam engine to generative AI. Pause after the fourth line and ask for a show of hands: who changed a daily habit because of AI this year? Use the answers to introduce the role cards.

How the Activity Runs

1
Role cards and briefing15 min

Groups of six to seven receive sealed role cards: founder/CEO, venture capital investor, chip and compute partner, US cloud partner, EU policy-maker (AI Act), open-source developer, and enterprise customer (for example a European bank). Each student reads their card, which lists a goal, a red line and one secret concern, and prepares three arguments.

2
Driver mapping20 min

Still in role, each group maps on a flipchart which drivers its actor controls: talent and expertise, compute and infrastructure, data, platforms and distribution, consumer habits, money and policy. Lines show who depends on whom. The result is a hand-drawn ecosystem map of Mistral AI.

3
The negotiation35 min

The founder must secure a deal that gives the venture compute, customers and capital while staying open-source and European. Other actors negotiate in rounds of five minutes, with one surprise event card per round (for example a new US model release, a chip shortage or an AI Act deadline). Agreements are written on index cards and signed.

4
Life-cycle check20 min

Groups step out of role and place Mistral AI on the Startup Life Cycle stages and on the Gartner Hype Cycle. They identify which social and which technical conditions must still change for the venture to reach the next stage, and present this in two minutes per group.

5
Debrief and reflection20 min

The lecturer compares the deals across groups and links them to the Multi-Level Perspective and to network effects. Students discuss how it felt to defend interests they do not personally share, then begin their individual written reflection.

Deliverable. Each group submits a photo of its hand-drawn ecosystem map and its signed deal cards, plus a two-minute oral life-cycle statement naming the next social and the next technical condition the venture needs.
