Pre-Reading: What Are You Really Buying From an Accelerator?

Key framework: Incubators vs Accelerators

Business Incubators in Entrepreneurship in Digitalization · Format: Case Study

Learning Focus · ILOs 2 & 3

Critically assess how incubators, accelerators, investors and IP protection create the conditions for a digital idea to become a venture, and reflect on your own role when stakeholders put you on the spot.

Key framework: Incubators vs Accelerators

Incubators offer open-ended, often subsidised support such as space and advice, while accelerators run fixed-term, cohort-based programmes with mentoring, usually a small investment for equity, and a final Demo Day. Knowing the difference helps founders choose support that fits their stage instead of joining the most famous brand.

Key Points

  • Incubators shelter very early ventures over a longer, open-ended period; accelerators compress learning into a few intensive months.
  • Accelerators usually take equity and end with a Demo Day pitch to investors.
  • The cohort (your batch-mates) is often as valuable as the mentors or the money.
  • Choose a programme by your stage and your bottleneck: customers, skills, network or capital.

Read Before Class

When Y Combinator started in 2005, it created a new kind of support organisation. Cohen and Hochberg (2014) describe the seed accelerator as a fixed-term, cohort-based programme with mentoring and education that ends in a public pitch event, the Demo Day. This is different from the classic business incubator, which offers office space, shared services and advice for as long as a young firm needs it (Hackett and Dilts, 2004). Incubators protect; accelerators compress.

Why does this matter for you? Because each model solves a different problem. Cohen (2013) argues that accelerators speed up learning by packing many interactions with mentors, peers and investors into a few months. An incubator suits a team that still needs time and cheap space to build its first prototype. An accelerator suits a team that already has something to test and wants fast feedback, investor access and a network.

The Langfuse case shows a hidden benefit: the batch itself. The founders came to Y Combinator with a billing tool, but conversations with other teams building AI products revealed a shared problem they could solve. Isenberg (2010) reminds us that an entrepreneurship ecosystem is made of many domains, including finance, culture, support organisations, human capital and markets. An accelerator concentrates several of these domains in one room for a short period.

Munich offers both models. UnternehmerTUM runs separate incubator and accelerator programmes, TUM Venture Labs support research-based start-ups, and WERK1 provides space, coaching and community for digital founders. Before you apply anywhere, ask three questions: What is my bottleneck right now? What will I give (equity, time, exclusivity)? And who will I meet that I cannot meet today?
