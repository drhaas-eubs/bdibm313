Pre-Reading: Why the Machine Is Never the Whole Revolution

Key framework: Multi-Level Perspective on socio-technical transitions

Main Drivers of the Digital Revolutions · Format: Role Play

Learning Focus · ILO 2

Critically assess the technical and social conditions, such as talent, compute, platforms, consumer habits and policy, that enable a digital revolution and allow digital ventures to emerge and grow.

Key framework: Multi-Level Perspective on socio-technical transitions

The Multi-Level Perspective explains big technological change as the interaction of three levels: niches where radical innovations are tried out, the socio-technical regime of established firms, rules, habits and infrastructures, and the wider landscape of slow-moving trends and shocks. A transition happens when landscape pressure weakens the regime and niche innovations are ready to take advantage (Geels, 2002).

Key Points

  • Niches are protected spaces, such as start-ups, research labs and early user communities, where radical innovations are developed.
  • The socio-technical regime is the established way of doing things: dominant firms, technologies, rules, user habits and infrastructure.
  • The landscape consists of broad trends and shocks, such as a pandemic, geopolitics or a new general purpose technology, that put pressure on the regime.
  • Digital entrepreneurs succeed when they align technical solutions with social change, not when they simply have the best technology.

Read Before Class

When people talk about digital revolutions, they often focus on machines: the steam engine, the internet, the smartphone, generative AI. Yet history shows that technology alone rarely changes the world. Perez (2002) argues that each technological revolution needs decades of social, financial and institutional change before its full benefits appear. The early digital businesses grew from an engineering culture that valued efficiency, automation and data, but they only became mainstream when consumers, regulators and other businesses changed their habits around them.

The Multi-Level Perspective helps you see this clearly (Geels, 2002). At the bottom are niches: small, protected spaces where new ideas are tested, such as start-ups, university labs and enthusiastic early users. In the middle is the socio-technical regime, the established system of firms, technologies, rules, infrastructure and user habits. At the top is the landscape: slow trends and sudden shocks, such as demographic change, a pandemic or rising geopolitical tension. Change happens when landscape pressure opens a window and niche innovations have become strong enough to use it.

Mistral AI illustrates the model. Its niche was open-weight language models built by a small team of experienced researchers. The regime was dominated by US technology giants with their own models, clouds and app stores. The landscape included the generative AI boom after ChatGPT and Europe’s growing concern about technological sovereignty. Mistral grew by connecting to regime actors, such as cloud and chip companies, while offering something the regime lacked: open models and a European alternative (Brynjolfsson and McAfee, 2014; Geels, 2002).

For a digital entrepreneur, the lesson is practical. Ask not only whether your technology works, but who has to change their behaviour for it to succeed, which established players can help or block you, and which wider trends are working for or against you. Before class, choose one digital service you started using recently and identify the niche it came from, the regime it challenges and the landscape trend that made you adopt it.
