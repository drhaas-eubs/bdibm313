Pre-Reading: Founders Are Not Born Thinking Like Founders

Key framework: Cognitive Adaptability

Sociological Considerations for Entrepreneurship in Digitalization · Format: Debate

Learning Focus · ILOs 2 & 3

Critically assess how culture, institutions, role models and support systems create the social conditions for digital entrepreneurship, and reflect on your own cognitive adaptability as a future founder.

Key framework: Cognitive Adaptability

Cognitive adaptability is the ability to be dynamic, flexible and self-regulating in how you think when the situation is uncertain and changing. It is not a fixed trait: it rests on metacognition — thinking about your own thinking — which can be practised and improved.

Key Points

  • Asking 'what personality do entrepreneurs have?' produced weak answers; asking what founders do and how they think is more useful.
  • Cognitive adaptability has five metacognitive dimensions: goal orientation, metacognitive knowledge, metacognitive experience, metacognitive choice and monitoring.
  • Thinking is situated: culture, institutions and support systems shape which strategies a founder even considers.
  • Role models and small mastery experiences raise entrepreneurial self-efficacy — the belief that you can do it.

Read Before Class

For decades researchers searched for the 'entrepreneurial personality': risk-loving, independent, driven. The results were disappointing. Gartner (1988) famously argued that 'Who is an entrepreneur?' is the wrong question and that we should study what entrepreneurs actually do. This matters for you. If founders were simply born, a course like this would be pointless. Research since then suggests something more hopeful: the way successful founders think can be described, diagnosed and trained, especially when they must act in markets that change faster than any plan.

Haynie and Shepherd (2009) call this capability cognitive adaptability: being dynamic, flexible and self-regulating in your thinking under uncertainty. It is built on metacognition, which means stepping back to think about how you are thinking. They describe five dimensions: setting goals (goal orientation), knowing your own strengths and blind spots (metacognitive knowledge), drawing on past experience (metacognitive experience), deliberately choosing between strategies (metacognitive choice) and checking whether the chosen approach is working (monitoring). A founder with high cognitive adaptability notices when a strategy fails and switches before the money runs out.

Thinking never happens in a vacuum. Haynie et al. (2010) describe the entrepreneurial mindset as situated: the environment shapes which options a founder even sees. A founder in Lagos faces different payment habits, logistics and regulation from a founder in Munich, and the same app idea demands different decisions. Jumia's reset after 2022 is a good example of leaders rethinking imported assumptions. Context also includes people. Role models show what is possible: Bosma et al. (2012) found that many entrepreneurs report role models, and that these mostly come from their close social environment.

How can you diagnose your own thinking? Before class, take a recent decision (choosing this university, a part-time job, a group project role) and ask: What was my goal? What did I already know about myself? Which experience did I rely on? Did I consider alternatives? Did I check whether it worked and adjust? Bandura (1997) showed that confidence grows through mastery experiences, observing others and encouragement. In the debate you will test whether context, mindset or both make a founder.
