Satish Nambisan

Putting digital technology at the heart of entrepreneurship

Satish Nambisan · Weatherhead School of Management, Case Western Reserve University

Digital entrepreneurship is shaped by digital artifacts, digital platforms and digital infrastructure, which make ventures less bounded and more open to many actors.

1. The “Why” — Why Satish Nambisan, Why Now?

Nambisan gives students a simple vocabulary to explain what is truly digital about a business. This is exactly what Assessment 1 asks when you compare a traditional business with its digital alternative.

2. Intellectual Foundation

Nambisan is the Nancy and Joseph Keithley Professor of Technology Management at Case Western Reserve University. He holds a PhD from Syracuse University and previously taught at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute and the University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee; his research covers innovation management, platforms and technology entrepreneurship.

3. The Frameworks · Tools · Models
Nambisan's Digital Entrepreneurship ElementsArtifacts, platforms and infrastructure as the building blocks of digital ventures

Nambisan's Digital Entrepreneurship Elements sheet →

4. Operationalisation — How to Use It
  • List every component of the business on sticky notes.
  • Label each note as artifact, platform or infrastructure, or as physical.
  • Circle the elements without which the customer value would disappear.
  • Mark which elements are owned by others, such as app stores or cloud providers.
  • Decide what to build, what to rent and where you are dependent.
[ Insert portrait sketch ]

Digital technologies make the boundaries of a venture and of its product fluid, so entrepreneurship becomes an ongoing, open process involving many actors rather than a single founder following a fixed plan.

Key idea — Satish Nambisan

5. Limitations — The Critical View
  • The article is conceptual and was written mainly as a research agenda, so it gives few practical tools.
  • The boundaries between artifact, platform and infrastructure can blur, for example when a platform becomes infrastructure for others.
  • It says little about the physical operations and costs that hybrid ventures still carry.
  • It focuses on technology and pays less attention to culture, regulation and finance.
6. Enduring Impact
  • One of the most cited articles in digital entrepreneurship research.
  • Established artifacts, platforms and infrastructure as a common vocabulary in the field.
  • Inspired later frameworks, including Buckley and Nzembayie’s typologies of pure and hybrid digital entrepreneurship.
  • Encouraged business schools to teach entrepreneurship from a digital technology perspective.

Hull, Hung, Hair, Perotti & DeMartino

Digital entrepreneurship is a matter of degree

Clyde Eiríkur Hull · Yu-Ting Caisy Hung · Neil Hair · Victor Perotti · Richard DeMartino · Rochester Institute of Technology

New digital ventures can be mild, moderate or extreme depending on how much of the product, distribution and workplace is digital.

1. The “Why” — Why Hull, Hung, Hair, Perotti & DeMartino, Why Now?

Their scale is the quickest way for bachelor students to position any business between traditional and fully digital. It helps you argue precisely rather than calling everything with an app digital.

2. Intellectual Foundation

The authors worked at the Saunders College of Business, Rochester Institute of Technology, when they published their typology in the International Journal of Networking and Virtual Organisations. Their work was an early academic answer to the question of how starting a digital venture differs from starting a traditional one.

3. The Frameworks · Tools · Models
Degrees of DigitizationA mild, moderate and extreme typology of digital ventures

Degrees of Digitization sheet →

4. Operationalisation — How to Use It
  • Describe the product or service and ask how much of it is digital.
  • Check whether marketing, sales and delivery happen online.
  • Check whether the team works in a digital or physical workplace.
  • Place the venture on the mild–moderate–extreme scale.
  • List the success factors and risks that follow from that position.
[ Insert portrait sketch ]

The more digital a venture is, the more its success depends on digital products, virtual workplaces and computer-mediated communication rather than on physical assets and face-to-face contact.

Key idea — Hull, Hung, Hair, Perotti & DeMartino

5. Limitations — The Critical View
  • Written in 2007, before smartphones, cloud computing and AI shaped digital business.
  • Three categories can oversimplify complex hybrid ventures.
  • The criteria for each degree are qualitative and open to interpretation.
  • Less widely cited and tested than other frameworks in this course.
6. Enduring Impact
  • One of the earliest typologies of digital entrepreneurship.
  • Frequently cited in literature reviews on digital entrepreneurship.
  • Helped establish digital entrepreneurship as a topic distinct from e-commerce.
  • Provides a simple tool for teaching and auditing businesses.

Selected Works

Taking advantage of digital opportunities: a typology of digital entrepreneurship

Jeffry A. Timmons

Entrepreneurship as a balancing act

Jeffry A. Timmons (1941–2008) · Babson College · Harvard Business School

Successful ventures keep opportunity, resources and team in balance, led by an entrepreneur who manages uncertainty.

1. The “Why” — Why Jeffry A. Timmons, Why Now?

Timmons reminds digital founders that even the best app needs a real opportunity and the right team. His model explains why money alone could not save Gorillas.

2. Intellectual Foundation

Timmons studied at Colgate University and earned his master’s and doctorate at Harvard Business School. He launched an early undergraduate entrepreneurship programme at Northeastern University and from 1982 taught at Babson College, with a parallel appointment at Harvard Business School from 1989 to 1995.

3. The Frameworks · Tools · Models
Timmons Model of the Entrepreneurial ProcessOpportunity, resources and team balanced by the lead entrepreneur

Timmons Model of the Entrepreneurial Process sheet →

4. Operationalisation — How to Use It
  • Describe the opportunity: customer need, market size and timing.
  • List the resources you have and those you still need.
  • Map the team’s skills and gaps.
  • Identify which of the three forces is out of balance.
  • Decide on one action that restores the fit this month.
[ Insert portrait sketch ]

Entrepreneurship starts with the opportunity, not with money or a business plan, and the founder’s main task is to keep opportunity, resources and team in fit as conditions change.

Key idea — Jeffry A. Timmons

5. Limitations — The Critical View
  • Developed for traditional ventures, before platforms and network effects.
  • Treats the opportunity as something to be found rather than created.
  • Says little about digital infrastructure or ecosystems.
  • The balance is easy to draw but hard to measure.
6. Enduring Impact
  • New Venture Creation became one of the most widely used entrepreneurship textbooks.
  • Shaped entrepreneurship curricula at Babson and many other business schools.
  • Popularised the opportunity–resources–team triangle used by incubators.
  • Helped make entrepreneurship a recognised academic field.

Sussan & Acs

Where digital and entrepreneurial ecosystems meet

Fiona Sussan · Zoltan J. Acs · George Mason University / London School of Economics

Digital entrepreneurship happens where infrastructure governance, user citizenship, entrepreneurs and digital marketplaces interact through multisided platforms.

1. The “Why” — Why Sussan & Acs, Why Now?

Their framework shows students that a digital venture never stands alone: it depends on platform rules, user rights and markets. This is central to ILO 2 on the conditions that enable digital entrepreneurship.

2. Intellectual Foundation

Zoltan J. Acs is a professor at the London School of Economics and George Mason University, a former Chief Economist of the US Small Business Administration and founder of the GEDI Institute. Together with Fiona Sussan he published the framework in Small Business Economics in 2017.

3. The Frameworks · Tools · Models
Digital Entrepreneurship EcosystemFour concepts linking infrastructure, users, entrepreneurs and marketplaces

Digital Entrepreneurship Ecosystem sheet →

4. Operationalisation — How to Use It
  • Identify who governs the digital infrastructure you rely on.
  • Describe how users participate, and which rights and data protection apply.
  • List the entrepreneurs and partners building on the same infrastructure.
  • Locate the marketplace where transactions happen.
  • Spot the weakest link and plan how to reduce your dependence on it.
[ Insert portrait sketch ]

To understand entrepreneurship in the digital age we must study agents and users together, because value is created through their interaction on multisided platforms within rules set by infrastructure governance.

Key idea — Sussan & Acs

5. Limitations — The Critical View
  • Conceptual framework with propositions rather than tested results.
  • Abstract terms such as digital user citizenship can be hard for practitioners to apply.
  • Pays limited attention to finance and the internal processes of start-ups.
  • Platform power has grown since 2017 and the framework does not fully capture it.
6. Enduring Impact
  • Highly cited starting point for research on digital entrepreneurial ecosystems.
  • Linked platform theory with entrepreneurship ecosystem research.
  • Helped policy-makers see users and data rules as part of the ecosystem.
  • Prompted follow-up studies that critique and extend the framework.

Selected Works

The digital entrepreneurial ecosystem

David L. Rogers

Digital transformation is a strategy question

David L. Rogers · Columbia Business School Executive Education

Digital technology rewrites the rules in five domains (customers, competition, data, innovation and value), so firms must rethink strategy, not just technology.

1. The “Why” — Why David L. Rogers, Why Now?

Rogers gives students a clear checklist for Assessment 1: how the customer, the competition, the data, innovation and value change when a traditional business goes digital.

2. Intellectual Foundation

Rogers is a faculty member at Columbia Business School, where he leads executive education programmes on digital business strategy. His books draw on his work with large companies on digital transformation and on research into customer networks.

3. The Frameworks · Tools · Models
Rogers' Five Domains of Digital TransformationCustomers, competition, data, innovation and value as the five domains of change

Rogers' Five Domains of Digital Transformation sheet →

4. Operationalisation — How to Use It
  • Write down the traditional assumption for each of the five domains.
  • Write the digital-age reality next to it.
  • Identify the biggest gap for your business.
  • Design one small experiment to close that gap.
  • Review the results and update the strategy.
[ Insert portrait sketch ]

“Digital transformation is not about technology — it is about strategy and new ways of thinking.”

— David L. Rogers

5. Limitations — The Critical View
  • Written mainly for large established companies rather than start-ups.
  • The five domains overlap, which can make analysis repetitive.
  • Gives limited guidance on financing transformation.
  • Examples date quickly as technology changes.
6. Enduring Impact
  • Widely used in executive education and MBA programmes on digital strategy.
  • Helped shift the debate from IT projects to strategic transformation.
  • Provides a simple structure for digital audits.
  • Extended into a practical roadmap for continuous change.

Clayton M. Christensen

Why great firms fail and small entrants win

Clayton M. Christensen (1952–2020) · Harvard Business School

Disruptive innovations start with simpler, cheaper offers for overlooked customers and move upmarket until they displace incumbents.

1. The “Why” — Why Clayton M. Christensen, Why Now?

Most digital start-ups enter markets dominated by big players. Christensen helps students judge whether a venture can really disrupt or is only competing head-on.

2. Intellectual Foundation

Christensen was a professor at Harvard Business School. His theory grew out of his doctoral research on the disk-drive industry and was first published with Joseph Bower in Harvard Business Review in 1995.

3. The Frameworks · Tools · Models
Disruptive InnovationLow-end or new-market entry that moves upmarket and displaces incumbents

Disruptive Innovation sheet →

4. Operationalisation — How to Use It
  • Find customers who are overserved or not served at all.
  • Design a simpler, cheaper or more convenient offer for them.
  • Check whether incumbents have an incentive to ignore you.
  • Improve the product step by step towards mainstream needs.
  • Watch for the moment mainstream customers switch.
[ Insert portrait sketch ]

“Disruption describes a process whereby a smaller company with fewer resources is able to successfully challenge established incumbent businesses.”

— Clayton M. Christensen

5. Limitations — The Critical View
  • The term is often misused for any new technology or start-up.
  • Critics argue that the theory is easier to apply in hindsight than to predict.
  • Some famous cases, such as Uber, do not fit the original definition well.
  • Pays less attention to network effects and platform dynamics.
6. Enduring Impact
  • One of the most influential management theories of the past 30 years.
  • Shaped how investors and founders talk about start-up strategy.
  • Prompted incumbents to set up separate innovation units.
  • Inspired further work such as Jobs-to-be-Done.

Westerman, Bonnet & McAfee

Digital masters combine technology and leadership

George Westerman · MIT Sloan School of Management · Didier Bonnet · Capgemini · Andrew McAfee · MIT

Digital maturity requires both digital capabilities and leadership capabilities; firms strong in both become Digital Masters.

1. The “Why” — Why Westerman, Bonnet & McAfee, Why Now?

The matrix helps students audit a traditional business and see that buying technology is not enough. It also links digital change to leadership, a key theme of ILO 3.

2. Intellectual Foundation

George Westerman is a Senior Lecturer at the MIT Sloan School of Management and a former research scientist at the MIT Center for Digital Business. The matrix comes from a multi-year study of large companies by MIT and Capgemini Consulting, published as Leading Digital in 2014.

3. The Frameworks · Tools · Models
Westerman Digital Maturity MatrixDigital and leadership capabilities define four levels of digital maturity

Westerman Digital Maturity Matrix sheet →

4. Operationalisation — How to Use It
  • Score the company on digital capabilities from low to high.
  • Score the company on leadership capabilities from low to high.
  • Place it in one of the four quadrants.
  • Identify which axis is weaker.
  • Plan two actions that move it towards Digital Master.
[ Insert portrait sketch ]

“When digital transformation is done right, it’s like a caterpillar turning into a butterfly, but when done wrong, all you have is a really fast caterpillar. ⚠”

— Westerman, Bonnet & McAfee

5. Limitations — The Critical View
  • Based on large companies, so less suited to early-stage start-ups.
  • Self-assessment of capabilities can be biased.
  • Four quadrants simplify a continuous journey.
  • Financial outperformance is correlated with maturity, not proven to be caused by it.
6. Enduring Impact
  • One of the most used digital maturity tools in consulting and teaching.
  • Made leadership capability a standard part of digital transformation discussions.
  • Leading Digital became a widely read management book.
  • Inspired many later maturity assessments.

Van Alstyne, Parker & Choudary

The new rules of platform strategy

Marshall W. Van Alstyne · Boston University · Geoffrey G. Parker · Dartmouth College · Sangeet Paul Choudary · Platform Thinking Labs

Pipelines create value through a linear chain of activities; platforms create value by facilitating interactions between external producers and consumers.

1. The “Why” — Why Van Alstyne, Parker & Choudary, Why Now?

Gorillas, Vinted and Too Good To Go show that the choice between owning a chain and orchestrating a network decides costs and growth. This framework helps students see that choice clearly.

2. Intellectual Foundation

Van Alstyne is a professor at Boston University Questrom School of Business and a research fellow at the MIT Initiative on the Digital Economy; Parker is a professor of engineering at Dartmouth College. Their academic work on two-sided network effects underpins the Harvard Business Review article written with platform strategist Sangeet Paul Choudary.

3. The Frameworks · Tools · Models
Pipelines vs PlatformsLinear value chains compared with networked platforms

Pipelines vs Platforms sheet →

4. Operationalisation — How to Use It
  • Draw your business as a chain and as a network.
  • Identify which assets you own and which you could orchestrate.
  • List outside producers who could add value.
  • Define the interaction you would facilitate.
  • Decide whether to run a pipeline, a platform or a hybrid.
[ Insert portrait sketch ]

“While plenty of pure pipeline businesses are still highly competitive, when platforms enter the same marketplace, the platforms virtually always win.”

— Van Alstyne, Parker & Choudary

5. Limitations — The Critical View
  • Overstates platform success; many platforms never reach critical mass.
  • Hybrid models, such as marketplaces with own stock, are common but less discussed.
  • Says little about regulation, labour conditions and platform power.
  • Examples focus on US technology giants.
6. Enduring Impact
  • Among the most read Harvard Business Review articles on platform strategy.
  • Popularised the pipeline versus platform language in business schools.
  • Linked network economics to everyday management decisions.
  • Influenced how investors evaluate marketplace start-ups.

Carlota Perez

Technological revolutions, bubbles and golden ages

Carlota Perez · UCL Institute for Innovation and Public Purpose · SPRU, University of Sussex · TalTech

Each technological revolution creates a new techno-economic paradigm that spreads through a financial frenzy, a turning point and a long period of deployment.

1. The “Why” — Why Carlota Perez, Why Now?

Perez helps students understand why AI and quick-commerce funding booms happen and why the real impact of a technology needs new institutions and habits. This is central to understanding the drivers of digital revolutions.

2. Intellectual Foundation

Perez is a British-Venezuelan scholar who studied at the University of Paris VII and San Francisco State University. She is Honorary Professor at UCL’s Institute for Innovation and Public Purpose and at SPRU, University of Sussex, and Adjunct Professor at TalTech in Estonia.

3. The Frameworks · Tools · Models
Techno-Economic ParadigmsFive technological revolutions, each moving from installation to deployment

Techno-Economic Paradigms sheet →

4. Operationalisation — How to Use It
  • Name the technological revolution your venture belongs to.
  • Decide whether the market is in installation or deployment.
  • Check how financial capital is behaving: frenzy or caution?
  • Identify which institutions and habits still need to change.
  • Time your launch and funding plan accordingly.
[ Insert portrait sketch ]

A technological revolution only delivers broad prosperity after society, finance and institutions adapt to it, which is why early bubbles are often followed by crashes and then long golden ages.

Key idea — Carlota Perez

5. Limitations — The Critical View
  • Long-wave theories are hard to test and date precisely.
  • Identifying the current phase in real time is difficult.
  • Focuses on the macro level, so offers limited guidance for individual start-ups.
  • Critics argue that history may not repeat in such regular patterns.
6. Enduring Impact
  • Widely read by venture capitalists and technology strategists.
  • Influenced debates on green growth and innovation policy.
  • Connected technology, finance and institutions in one framework.
  • Recognised by Prospect magazine among the world’s top thinkers in 2020.

Selected Works

Everett M. Rogers

How new ideas spread through society

Everett M. Rogers (1931–2004) · communication scholar · University of New Mexico

Innovations spread through communication channels over time among members of a social system, from innovators to laggards.

1. The “Why” — Why Everett M. Rogers, Why Now?

Rogers explains why early adopters matter so much for digital ventures. It helps students plan who to win first and how a new habit can spread to the mass market.

2. Intellectual Foundation

Rogers was an American sociologist and communication scholar who grew up on a farm in Iowa and studied at Iowa State University, where he researched how farmers adopted new agricultural practices. His book Diffusion of Innovations, first published in 1962, went through five editions.

3. The Frameworks · Tools · Models
Diffusion of InnovationsFive adopter categories and five attributes that shape adoption speed

Diffusion of Innovations sheet →

4. Operationalisation — How to Use It
  • Describe your innovators and early adopters precisely.
  • Rate your product on relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability and observability.
  • Fix the weakest attribute first.
  • Make results visible so early adopters can persuade others.
  • Plan the move from early adopters to the early majority.
[ Insert portrait sketch ]

“Diffusion is the process in which an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time among the members of a social system.”

— Everett M. Rogers

5. Limitations — The Critical View
  • Pro-innovation bias: assumes that adoption is always good.
  • The bell curve percentages are idealised, not observed in every market.
  • Places less emphasis on network effects and platform dynamics.
  • Mostly descriptive rather than predictive for individual products.
6. Enduring Impact
  • One of the most cited works in the social sciences.
  • The adopter categories became standard vocabulary in marketing.
  • Basis for Moore’s Crossing the Chasm.
  • Applied in public health, agriculture, education and technology.

Selected Works

Fenn & Raskino

Timing innovation beyond the hype

Jackie Fenn · Mark Raskino · Gartner

Emerging technologies move through a predictable cycle of inflated expectations, disillusionment and gradual productive use.

1. The “Why” — Why Fenn & Raskino, Why Now?

Students will found or join ventures built on hyped technologies such as AI. The hype cycle helps them judge timing and avoid confusing media attention with market demand.

2. Intellectual Foundation

Jackie Fenn joined Gartner in 1994 and introduced the hype cycle in 1995; it has since been published by Gartner every year. With fellow Gartner analyst Mark Raskino she explained the method in Mastering the Hype Cycle (2008).

3. The Frameworks · Tools · Models
Gartner Hype CycleFive phases from innovation trigger to plateau of productivity

Gartner Hype Cycle sheet →

4. Operationalisation — How to Use It
  • Collect evidence on media attention, funding and real use.
  • Place the technology on the curve.
  • Decide whether to wait, test or invest.
  • Look for proven use cases that survive the trough.
  • Review the position every six months.
[ Insert portrait sketch ]

Organisations should neither chase hype at its peak nor abandon a technology in the trough, but adopt it when it fits their own needs and evidence of value is growing.

Key idea — Fenn & Raskino

5. Limitations — The Critical View
  • Not based on a rigorous scientific method.
  • Many technologies do not follow the full curve.
  • Positions on the curve are subjective judgements by analysts.
  • Offers limited guidance on what to do at each phase.
6. Enduring Impact
  • One of the best-known images in technology management.
  • Published annually by Gartner across many technology areas.
  • Made the trough of disillusionment a common business term.
  • Used by investors and managers to discuss technology timing.

Selected Works

Mastering the Hype Cycle: How to Choose the Right Innovation at the Right Time
Book by Fenn and Raskino
2008

Brynjolfsson & McAfee

The second machine age of brilliant technologies

Erik Brynjolfsson · Stanford Digital Economy Lab · Andrew McAfee · MIT Initiative on the Digital Economy

Digital technologies multiply mental power through exponential growth, digitisation and combinatorial innovation.

1. The “Why” — Why Brynjolfsson & McAfee, Why Now?

Their forces help students explain why AI ventures like Mistral can grow so fast, and why the benefits and costs of digital technology are not shared equally.

2. Intellectual Foundation

Brynjolfsson is Director of the Stanford Digital Economy Lab and a Senior Fellow at Stanford’s Institute for Human-Centered AI; McAfee is co-founder and co-director of the MIT Initiative on the Digital Economy and a principal research scientist at MIT Sloan. Both are leading researchers on technology, productivity and work.

3. The Frameworks · Tools · Models
The Second Machine Age forcesExponential, digital and combinatorial forces of digital change

The Second Machine Age forces sheet →

4. Operationalisation — How to Use It
  • List the technologies that improve exponentially in your field.
  • Identify which activities and data are newly digitised.
  • Look for existing building blocks you can recombine.
  • Sketch a product that combines all three forces.
  • Consider who wins and who loses from the change.
[ Insert portrait sketch ]

“Computers and other digital advances are doing for mental power—the ability to use our brains to understand and shape our environments—what the steam engine and its descendants did for muscle power.”

— Brynjolfsson & McAfee

5. Limitations — The Critical View
  • Strongly US-centred examples.
  • Productivity gains predicted by the authors have been slow to appear in statistics.
