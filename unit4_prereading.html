Pre-Reading: Nineteen Channels, One Budget

Key framework: Bullseye Traction Framework

Acting on Digital Entrepreneurial Forces · Format: Business Challenge

Learning Focus · ILOs 1 & 3

Investigate how digital ventures turn opportunities into strategy, budgets and channel choices, and evaluate the entrepreneurial management decisions and leadership needed to allocate scarce resources under pressure.

Key framework: Bullseye Traction Framework

The Bullseye Framework helps start-ups find the marketing channel that will bring their first real growth. Founders brainstorm all 19 traction channels, test the most promising few cheaply, and then focus their budget on the one that works.

Key Points

  • Most start-ups fail because they do not get enough customers, not because they lack a product.
  • There are 19 possible traction channels, from SEO and social ads to PR, sales and offline events.
  • The Bullseye moves from outer ring (brainstorm all) to middle ring (test a few) to inner ring (focus on one).
  • A channel is only good if its customer acquisition cost is clearly lower than customer lifetime value.

Read Before Class

Founders love building products, but customers do not appear by themselves. Weinberg and Mares (2015) argue that most failed start-ups had a product but not enough customers. Their answer is to treat traction, meaning measurable customer growth, as seriously as product development. They identify 19 traction channels, including search engine marketing, social and display ads, offline ads, SEO, content marketing, email, viral marketing, PR, unconventional PR, business development, sales, affiliate programmes, existing platforms, trade shows, offline events, speaking engagements, community building, targeting blogs and engineering as marketing.

The Bullseye Framework turns this long list into a decision. In the outer ring, the team brainstorms one realistic idea for every channel. In the middle ring, it picks the three most promising and runs cheap tests, for example €300 of ads or ten sales calls. In the inner ring, it focuses on the single channel that delivered customers at the best cost. When that channel stops working, the team repeats the process (Weinberg and Mares, 2015).

How do you know a channel works? Compare what it costs to acquire a customer with what that customer is worth over time. Customer lifetime value research shows that the value of a customer base depends on acquisition cost, margin and retention (Gupta, Lehmann and Stuart, 2004). If you pay €40 to acquire a user who brings €25 of margin before leaving, the channel destroys value, however many downloads it creates.

Channels also work together. Paid media (ads) can create attention, owned media (your app, website, newsletter) keeps it, and earned media (reviews, word of mouth, press) multiplies it (Corcoran, 2009). Vinted shows this mix in practice: it used TV, a digital-only brand's surprising choice, to build trust in second-hand fashion, while satisfied users spread the word (Gollasch, 2025). Your strategy is the choice of where to put your first euro.
