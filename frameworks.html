Nambisan's Digital Entrepreneurship Elements

A three-part lens to see how digital a venture really is

SLIBRARY 01 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A conceptual lens that explains digital entrepreneurship through three distinct but related elements: digital artifacts (software-based components, applications and content), digital platforms (shared sets of services and architecture that host complementary offerings) and digital infrastructure (digital tools and systems that support collaboration, communication and innovation). It shows that digital technologies make entrepreneurial processes and outcomes less bounded and the locus of entrepreneurial agency less predefined (Nambisan, 2017).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Satish Nambisan, Professor of Technology Management at the Weatherhead School of Management, Case Western Reserve University, proposed the lens in a widely cited article (Nambisan, 2017). It draws on his earlier research into digital innovation and platforms.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Entrepreneurship theory had largely been built on physical products and fixed organisations, so it struggled to explain ventures whose products are never finished and whose teams are spread across platforms. Nambisan wanted researchers and founders to treat digital technology as a central part of entrepreneurship, not just a background tool.

FIGURE 1 · NAMBISAN'S DIGITAL ENTREPRENEURSHIP ELEMENTS

Figure 1. Artifacts, platforms, infrastructure: the three building blocks of digital ventures.

HOW IT IS USED

List every component of a business and label it as artifact, platform or infrastructure. Then ask which of these is core to the customer value and which only supports it, and identify where the venture depends on platforms or infrastructure owned by others.

WHEN IT IS USED

At the idea stage to clarify what is genuinely digital about a concept, and later when deciding which elements to build, rent or partner for.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, students preparing a digital audit, investors and researchers analysing digital ventures.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Spotify’s app and recommendation algorithm are digital artifacts, its marketplace for artists and podcasters is a platform, and it runs on cloud services and mobile networks as infrastructure.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier entrepreneurship models treated the venture and its product as clearly bounded units. Nambisan (2017) showed that digital artifacts and platforms make both the outcome and the actors fluid, with users and partners co-creating value.

Degrees of Digitization

A simple scale from digitally supported to fully digital businesses

SLIBRARY 01 · SHEET 2 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A typology that classifies new digital ventures as mild, moderate or extreme according to how far their products, services, distribution and workplace are digital. The degree of digitization shapes the success factors of a venture, including its reliance on computer-mediated communication and virtual workplaces (Hull et al., 2007).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Clyde Eiríkur Hull, Yu-Ting Caisy Hung, Neil Hair, Victor Perotti and Richard DeMartino of the Rochester Institute of Technology developed the typology (Hull et al., 2007). It was one of the first academic attempts to distinguish digital from traditional start-ups.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

As more companies started doing business online, it was unclear how starting a digital venture differs from starting a traditional one. The authors wanted a practical way to compare ventures and their different success factors.

FIGURE 1 · DEGREES OF DIGITIZATION

Figure 1. Mild, moderate or extreme: how much of the business is digital?

HOW IT IS USED

Check how digital the product or service, the marketing and sales, the delivery and the workplace are. Place the venture at mild, moderate or extreme, then discuss which skills, costs and risks follow from that position.

WHEN IT IS USED

When describing a business idea or auditing an existing company, especially to explain how far digital transformation could or should go.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Students, founders, consultants and lecturers comparing traditional, hybrid and pure digital businesses.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A Munich bakery with online pre-orders is mildly digital, Too Good To Go is moderately digital because the food is physical, and a language app such as Babbel is extremely digital.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Before this typology, firms were usually labelled simply as online or offline. Hull et al. (2007) introduced a graded scale, showing that digital entrepreneurship is a matter of degree rather than a yes-or-no category.

Timmons Model of the Entrepreneurial Process

Opportunity, resources and team kept in balance by the lead entrepreneur

SLIBRARY 01 · SHEET 3 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A model that describes entrepreneurship as a dynamic balancing act between three driving forces: the opportunity, the resources and the team. The lead entrepreneur keeps these forces in fit under conditions of ambiguity, uncertainty and changing external forces, with the opportunity as the starting point (Spinelli and Adams, 2016).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Jeffry A. Timmons (1941–2008), a pioneer of entrepreneurship education at Babson College and Harvard Business School, developed the model in his textbook New Venture Creation. Later editions were continued by Stephen Spinelli and Rob Adams (Spinelli and Adams, 2016).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Early thinking focused mainly on the personality of the entrepreneur or on the business plan. Timmons wanted a teachable model that showed how founders must constantly rebalance opportunity, money and people as a venture grows.

FIGURE 1 · TIMMONS MODEL OF THE ENTREPRENEURIAL PROCESS

Figure 1. Balance opportunity, resources and team, led by the founder.

HOW IT IS USED

Describe the opportunity in terms of customer need and market size, list the resources you have and need, and map the team’s skills. Identify which force is currently too weak or too large and decide what to change first.

WHEN IT IS USED

From idea to growth stage, particularly before fundraising or when a venture feels out of balance.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, entrepreneurship students, incubator coaches and early-stage investors.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Gorillas had a large team and huge resources in 2021, but the opportunity (profitable ten-minute delivery) proved smaller than assumed, so the model tipped out of balance.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Instead of a linear plan, Timmons framed entrepreneurship as a continuous balancing of three forces. This made it easier to see why ventures with plenty of capital, like Gorillas, can still fail when opportunity and resources fall out of fit (Spinelli and Adams, 2016).

UNIT 1 · SLIBRARY 01 — DIGITAL ENTREPRENEURSHIP FOUNDATIONS

Digital Entrepreneurship Ecosystem

Four connected arenas where digital entrepreneurs, users and rules meet

SLIBRARY 01 · SHEET 4 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A framework that integrates the digital ecosystem and the entrepreneurial ecosystem to explain how agents and users interact in the digital age. It consists of four concepts: digital infrastructure governance, digital user citizenship, digital entrepreneurship and the digital marketplace, connected through multisided platforms (Sussan and Acs, 2017).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Fiona Sussan and Zoltan J. Acs developed the framework (Sussan and Acs, 2017). Acs is a leading entrepreneurship economist, linked with George Mason University and the London School of Economics, and co-creator of the Global Entrepreneurship and Development Index.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Research on entrepreneurial ecosystems focused on regions and institutions, while research on digital ecosystems focused on technology and users. The authors saw a gap in explaining entrepreneurship that happens on digital platforms with millions of users.

FIGURE 1 · DIGITAL ENTREPRENEURSHIP ECOSYSTEM

Figure 1. Infrastructure, users, entrepreneurs and markets interact around platforms.

HOW IT IS USED

Map who governs the infrastructure (for example app stores or regulators), how users participate and are protected, which entrepreneurs build on the infrastructure and where the transactions happen. Then look for bottlenecks and opportunities between the four areas.

WHEN IT IS USED

When choosing where to build a digital venture, which platforms to rely on and how regulation and user behaviour may affect growth.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Policy-makers, ecosystem managers, founders and researchers.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A Munich app developer depends on Apple’s App Store rules (infrastructure governance), on user trust and data rights under the GDPR (user citizenship) and on the store as a marketplace.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier ecosystem models looked at entrepreneurs or technology separately. Sussan and Acs (2017) combined them and placed users and multisided platforms at the centre.

UNIT 1 · SLIBRARY 02 — TRADITIONAL VS DIGITAL BUSINESS

Rogers' Five Domains of Digital Transformation

Five areas where digital technology rewrites the rules of strategy

SLIBRARY 02 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A strategic framework stating that digital technologies change the rules of business in five domains: customers, competition, data, innovation and value. Firms transform successfully when they rethink their strategy in all five domains rather than simply upgrading their technology (Rogers, 2016).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

David L. Rogers, a faculty member of Columbia Business School Executive Education, developed the framework in The Digital Transformation Playbook (Rogers, 2016). He later extended it in The Digital Transformation Roadmap (Rogers, 2023).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Many established companies treated digital as an IT project and failed to adapt. Rogers wanted a strategic tool that helps managers see how customer behaviour, competition and value creation shift in the digital age.

FIGURE 1 · ROGERS' FIVE DOMAINS OF DIGITAL TRANSFORMATION

Figure 1. Customers, competition, data, innovation, value: rethink all five.

HOW IT IS USED

For each domain, compare the traditional assumption with the digital reality, for example customers as a mass market versus customers as a network. Then choose one or two strategic moves per domain and test them.

WHEN IT IS USED

When an existing business plans its digital transformation, or when a start-up wants to attack an incumbent.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Managers, consultants, students auditing a traditional business and founders challenging incumbents.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Vinted treats its customers as a network of sellers and buyers, uses data to price and match items and earns value from buyer fees rather than from owning stock.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Previous approaches focused on technology adoption and process efficiency. Rogers (2016) shifted the focus to strategy and showed that the core assumptions of business, not only its tools, must change.

Disruptive Innovation

How simpler, cheaper entrants overtake established market leaders

SLIBRARY 02 · SHEET 2 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Disruptive innovation describes a process whereby a smaller company with fewer resources successfully challenges established incumbents, typically by targeting overlooked low-end or new-market customers with simpler, cheaper offerings and then moving upmarket. Incumbents often ignore the entrant because they focus on improving products for their most profitable customers (Christensen, Raynor and McDonald, 2015).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Clayton M. Christensen (1952–2020), Professor at Harvard Business School, introduced the theory with Joseph Bower (Bower and Christensen, 1995) and developed it in The Innovator’s Dilemma (Christensen, 1997).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Christensen wanted to explain why well-managed, successful firms lose their leadership. He found that doing the right things for current customers can blind incumbents to new technologies that start out as inferior.

FIGURE 1 · DISRUPTIVE INNOVATION

Figure 1. Small entrants start at the bottom and move upmarket.

HOW IT IS USED

Identify customers who are overserved or not served at all, offer them a simpler, cheaper or more convenient solution and improve it until it is good enough for mainstream customers. Incumbents can use the same lens to spot threats early.

WHEN IT IS USED

When entering a market dominated by large players, or when an established firm evaluates new low-cost competitors.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, strategists, investors and corporate innovation teams.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

N26 started with a simple mobile account for digitally native young customers whom traditional banks found unattractive, then added premium accounts and services.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier innovation theories stressed breakthrough technology. Christensen (1997) showed that the business model and the customer segment, not the technology itself, make an innovation disruptive.

Westerman Digital Maturity Matrix

Four levels of digital maturity: Beginners, Fashionistas, Conservatives, Masters

SLIBRARY 02 · SHEET 3 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A two-by-two matrix that classifies organisations by their digital capabilities (what technology-enabled initiatives they invest in) and their leadership capabilities (how they envision, govern and lead transformation). It distinguishes Beginners, Fashionistas, Conservatives and Digital Masters, the last of which combine both capabilities and tend to outperform their peers financially (Westerman, Bonnet and McAfee, 2014).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

George Westerman of the MIT Sloan School of Management, Didier Bonnet of Capgemini and Andrew McAfee of MIT developed the matrix from a multi-year study of large companies by the MIT Center for Digital Business and Capgemini Consulting (Westerman, Bonnet and McAfee, 2014).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Companies were spending heavily on technology without seeing results. The researchers wanted to show that technology spending pays off only when it is matched by strong leadership and transformation management.

FIGURE 1 · WESTERMAN DIGITAL MATURITY MATRIX

Figure 1. Digital tools plus leadership make a digital master.

HOW IT IS USED

Rate a company from low to high on digital capabilities and on leadership capabilities, place it in one of the four quadrants and identify the next move: Fashionistas need more governance, Conservatives need bolder digital initiatives.

WHEN IT IS USED

In a digital audit of an established business, or when a growing start-up checks whether its leadership keeps up with its technology.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Executives, consultants, students preparing Assessment 1 and board members.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A Munich fashion retailer that launches an app, TikTok shop and AI chatbot without a clear vision or governance is a Fashionista; Zalando, which ties its platform strategy to leadership and data, is closer to a Digital Master.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier maturity models measured technology adoption alone. Westerman, Bonnet and McAfee (2014) added leadership capability as an equal second axis.

UNIT 1 · SLIBRARY 02 — TRADITIONAL VS DIGITAL BUSINESS

Pipelines vs Platforms

Linear value chains versus networks that orchestrate external producers and users

SLIBRARY 02 · SHEET 4 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A strategic distinction between pipeline businesses, which create value by controlling a linear series of activities from inputs to customers, and platform businesses, which create value by facilitating interactions between external producers and consumers. Platforms shift strategy from controlling resources to orchestrating them, from optimising internal processes to facilitating external interactions and from customer value to ecosystem value (Van Alstyne, Parker and Choudary, 2016).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Marshall W. Van Alstyne (Boston University), Geoffrey G. Parker (Dartmouth College) and Sangeet Paul Choudary (Platform Thinking Labs) set out the distinction in Harvard Business Review (Van Alstyne, Parker and Choudary, 2016).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Traditional strategy tools, such as Porter’s value chain, could not explain why companies like Apple, Airbnb and Uber overtook strong incumbents. The authors wanted to show managers that platforms follow different rules.

FIGURE 1 · PIPELINES VS PLATFORMS

Figure 1. Pipelines push products down a chain; platforms connect producers and consumers.

HOW IT IS USED

Draw the business as either a chain (supplier, firm, customer) or a network (producers, platform, consumers). Ask who creates value, who owns the assets and how the business would grow if outside producers did most of the work.

WHEN IT IS USED

When designing a business model, choosing between building stock and building a marketplace, or analysing a competitor.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, strategists, product managers and investors.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Gorillas owned its stock, stores and riders like a pipeline, whereas Too Good To Go connects existing bakeries and restaurants with consumers without owning the food.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Classic strategy focused on controlling scarce resources along a value chain. Van Alstyne, Parker and Choudary (2016) showed that platforms win by orchestrating resources they do not own and by harnessing network effects.

UNIT 2 · SLIBRARY 03 — TECHNOLOGY WAVES & DIFFUSION

Techno-Economic Paradigms

How technological revolutions move from bubbles to golden ages

SLIBRARY 03 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A theory of long waves stating that capitalism has passed through successive technological revolutions, each creating a new techno-economic paradigm, a set of best practices that reshapes the whole economy. Each surge passes through an installation period driven by financial capital and speculative frenzy, a turning point often marked by a crash, and a deployment period in which production capital and institutions spread the benefits widely (Perez, 2002).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Carlota Perez, a British-Venezuelan scholar and Honorary Professor at UCL’s Institute for Innovation and Public Purpose and at SPRU, University of Sussex, developed the theory (Perez, 2002). She built on the long-wave tradition of Joseph Schumpeter and Christopher Freeman.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Economists struggled to explain why major technologies arrive with bubbles and crises before producing lasting prosperity. Perez wanted to link technology, finance and institutions in one model of recurring change.

FIGURE 1 · TECHNO-ECONOMIC PARADIGMS

Figure 1. Each technological revolution: installation frenzy, turning point, then deployment.

HOW IT IS USED

Identify the current technological revolution and its key inputs, such as chips, data or AI. Ask whether the market is in installation (speculation, rapid start-up creation) or deployment (institutional adaptation, broad use), and adjust strategy, funding and timing accordingly.

WHEN IT IS USED

When judging the timing of a digital venture, understanding funding booms and busts, or explaining why regulation and habits lag behind technology.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Strategists, investors, policy-makers and founders thinking about long-term timing.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

The quick-commerce boom of 2021 and the generative AI funding surge after 2023 both show installation-period behaviour, with huge capital flows into young start-ups before business models are proven.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier long-wave theories focused on technology and economic cycles. Perez (2002) added the central role of financial capital and institutions and explained the recurring pattern of bubble, crash and golden age.

UNIT 2 · SLIBRARY 03 — TECHNOLOGY WAVES & DIFFUSION

Diffusion of Innovations

Who adopts new technology first, and what makes it spread

SLIBRARY 03 · SHEET 2 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Diffusion is the process in which an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time among the members of a social system. Adopters fall into five categories, innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority and laggards, and the speed of adoption depends on perceived attributes such as relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability and observability (Rogers, 2003).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Everett M. Rogers (1931–2004), an American communication scholar, developed the theory from studies of how farmers adopted new practices and published it in Diffusion of Innovations, first in 1962 (Rogers, 2003). He later taught at the University of New Mexico among other universities.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Rogers wanted to explain why some useful innovations spread quickly while others are ignored for years. He showed that adoption is a social process, shaped by communication and networks, not only by technical quality.

Network Effects

Why digital markets tip towards a few winners

SLIBRARY 04 · SHEET 2 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Network effects (network externalities) exist when the value a user gains from a product increases with the number of other users of that product or of compatible products. They create positive feedback, so markets with strong network effects tend to tip towards one or a few dominant players (Katz and Shapiro, 1985; Shapiro and Varian, 1999).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Economists Michael L. Katz and Carl Shapiro formalised network externalities in the American Economic Review (Katz and Shapiro, 1985). Carl Shapiro and Hal R. Varian of the University of California, Berkeley, translated the idea into business strategy in Information Rules (Shapiro and Varian, 1999).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Classic economics assumed that value comes from the product alone. Katz and Shapiro wanted to explain competition and compatibility in industries such as telephones and computers, where value depends on how many others use the same system.

FIGURE 1 · NETWORK EFFECTS

Figure 1. Each new user makes the product more valuable for everyone.

HOW IT IS USED

Identify whether your network effects are same-side (more users attract more users) or cross-side (more buyers attract more sellers). Solve the chicken-and-egg problem by seeding one side first and protect the network through compatibility and quality.

WHEN IT IS USED

When launching a platform, choosing a growth strategy, or judging whether a market will become winner-takes-all.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, investors, competition authorities and strategists.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

The more developers build tools and fine-tuned versions of Mistral’s open models, the more attractive those models become for the next developer and company.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier economics treated demand as independent of other buyers. Katz and Shapiro (1985) showed that consumption externalities shape competition and standards, and Shapiro and Varian (1999) turned this into practical strategy for information goods.

Niches, regimes and landscape: how whole systems change

SLIBRARY 04 · SHEET 3 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A framework that explains technological transitions as changes in socio-technical configurations resulting from the interplay of three levels: niches, where radical innovations emerge; socio-technical regimes, the stable rules, technologies, practices and actors of a sector; and the socio-technical landscape of broad external trends. Transitions occur when niche innovations build momentum while landscape pressures destabilise the regime (Geels, 2002).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Frank W. Geels, Professor of System Innovation and Sustainability at the University of Manchester, developed the framework in his doctoral research at the University of Twente and published it in Research Policy (Geels, 2002).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Studies of innovation often focused on single technologies or firms and could not explain how whole systems, such as transport or energy, change. Geels wanted to show that transitions involve technology, users, markets, policy and culture together.

HOW IT IS USED

Identify the niche innovation, describe the regime it challenges (incumbents, rules, habits, infrastructure) and list the landscape trends that create pressure. Then judge whether the niche is ready and which regime actors could become allies.

WHEN IT IS USED

When assessing whether a digital innovation can change an entire sector, and how long that change might take.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Researchers, policy-makers, sustainability strategists and founders working on system-level change.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Open-weight AI models (niche) challenge the closed-model regime of US technology giants, helped by landscape pressure such as Europe’s push for digital sovereignty.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier innovation studies treated technology change as a firm-level or market-level event. Geels (2002) introduced a multi-level, socio-technical view that links technical change with social and institutional change.

The feedback loop that turns start-up guesses into validated learning

SLIBRARY 05 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

The Build–Measure–Learn loop is the core feedback cycle of the Lean Startup method, in which a venture turns ideas into a minimum viable product, measures how customers respond and uses the data to decide whether to pivot or persevere. Progress is measured in validated learning rather than in features delivered (Ries, 2011).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Eric Ries, an American entrepreneur and co-founder of the virtual-world company IMVU, developed the method from his own start-up experience and from lean manufacturing ideas (Ries, 2011). He built on Steve Blank's customer development approach.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Many start-ups failed after spending years building products nobody wanted. Ries wanted a scientific way for founders to work under extreme uncertainty and to waste less time and money.

FIGURE 1 · LEAN STARTUP BUILD–MEASURE–LEARN

Figure 1. Build small, measure real behaviour, learn, then pivot or persevere.

HOW IT IS USED

Write down your leap-of-faith assumptions, build the smallest product that can test them, and define one actionable metric before launch. Run the test, compare the result with your prediction and decide: pivot or persevere.

WHEN IT IS USED

From the very first idea through early growth, whenever a key assumption about customers, value or revenue is still untested.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, product managers and innovation teams in start-ups and in established firms.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Lovable users can generate a working app in hours, test it with ten target users and change the product the next day, running several Build–Measure–Learn loops in a single week.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier planning tools assumed founders could predict the market and execute a fixed plan. Ries (2011) replaced the plan with a short experimental loop and introduced validated learning as the unit of progress.

Customer Development

Four steps to search for a repeatable, scalable business model

SLIBRARY 05 · SHEET 2 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Customer Development is a four-step process for start-ups (customer discovery, customer validation, customer creation and company building) that runs alongside product development and treats the business model as a set of hypotheses to be tested directly with customers. A start-up is seen as a temporary organisation searching for a repeatable and scalable business model (Blank and Dorf, 2012).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Steve Blank, a Silicon Valley serial entrepreneur and lecturer at Stanford and UC Berkeley, developed Customer Development from his own start-up experience (Blank and Dorf, 2012). It became a foundation of the Lean Startup movement.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Start-ups traditionally followed a product development process borrowed from large companies, building first and selling later. Blank saw that most failures came from a lack of customers, not a lack of products.

FIGURE 1 · CUSTOMER DEVELOPMENT

Figure 1. Get out of the building: customers hold the facts.

HOW IT IS USED

Write your business model hypotheses, then leave the office to interview and test with real customers (discovery). Prove that customers will buy (validation) before spending on marketing (creation) and building the full organisation (company building).

WHEN IT IS USED

Before and during MVP development, and again whenever the venture enters a new market or segment.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Early-stage founders, university spin-off teams and corporate innovation units.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Before building a student flat-sharing app, a team interviews 20 international students in Munich and discovers that the real pain is deposit fraud, not finding listings, and changes its value proposition.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Blank separated searching for a business model from executing one, a distinction missing in classic business-planning approaches. His 'get out of the building' rule made customer contact the first activity of a start-up (Blank, 2013).

Effectuation

How expert entrepreneurs decide when the future cannot be predicted

SLIBRARY 05 · SHEET 3 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Effectuation is a logic of entrepreneurial decision-making in which founders start from the means they already control (who they are, what they know and whom they know) and co-create possible outcomes with partners, rather than first setting a goal and then predicting the best path to it (causation). It focuses on what can be controlled instead of what can be predicted (Sarasvathy, 2001).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Saras Sarasvathy, professor at the Darden School of Business, University of Virginia, developed effectuation by studying how expert entrepreneurs reasoned through business problems (Sarasvathy, 2001; 2008).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Classic management theory assumes goals are given and the future can be forecast. Sarasvathy wanted to explain how experienced entrepreneurs create new markets when neither goals nor markets exist yet.

FIGURE 1 · EFFECTUATION

Figure 1. Start with what you have, not with a perfect forecast.

HOW IT IS USED

List your means (identity, knowledge, network), decide how much you can afford to lose, and approach people who could commit resources. Treat surprises as opportunities and let partners help shape the venture.

WHEN IT IS USED

At the earliest stage of a venture, when uncertainty is high and there is little market data.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

First-time and expert founders, student entrepreneurs and anyone starting with limited resources.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A student who speaks three languages and knows local restaurant owners starts a menu-translation service for them, using a free AI tool and an affordable loss of 200 euros, instead of writing a full plan first.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Effectuation reversed the causal logic of business planning: instead of goal first, means second, it starts with means and lets goals emerge (Sarasvathy, 2001). It gave a scientific basis to 'learning by doing' in entrepreneurship.

A six-lens scan of the macro-environment around a digital venture

SLIBRARY 05 · SHEET 4 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

PESTEL analysis is a structured scan of the macro-environment of an organisation across political, economic, social, technological, environmental and legal factors. It grew out of Aguilar's work on how managers scan their business environment for relevant external information (Aguilar, 1967).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Francis J. Aguilar of Harvard Business School introduced environmental scanning and a four-factor version (economic, technical, political, social) in 1967 (Aguilar, 1967). Later authors added environmental and legal factors.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Managers were overwhelmed by external information and lacked a systematic way to spot trends that could affect their business. The framework gave scanning a simple structure.

FIGURE 1 · PESTEL ANALYSIS

Figure 1. Scan six outside forces before they scan you.

HOW IT IS USED

For each of the six factors, list current trends and rate their likely impact on your venture (positive or negative, high or low). Turn the top three into opportunities or threats for your SWOT/TOWS analysis.

WHEN IT IS USED

Before choosing a market or launching in a new country, and at least yearly as part of strategic planning.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, strategy teams, consultants and investors doing market research.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A Munich food-delivery start-up checks the EU platform-work rules (legal), inflation (economic), demand for sustainable packaging (environmental) and AI route planning (technological) before choosing its launch city.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Aguilar (1967) moved environmental analysis from informal intuition to a systematic scanning process. The later PESTEL version added legal and environmental factors that matter strongly for digital ventures, such as data protection and sustainability rules.

The Business Model Canvas is a visual template that describes a business model through nine building blocks: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships and cost structure. It shows how an organisation creates, delivers and captures value (Osterwalder and Pigneur, 2010).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Alexander Osterwalder developed the business model ontology in his doctoral research at the University of Lausanne, supervised by Yves Pigneur, and the two turned it into the canvas (Osterwalder and Pigneur, 2010).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Managers talked about business models without a shared language, and business plans were long and hard to change. The canvas created a common, visual vocabulary teams can use together.

FIGURE 1 · BUSINESS MODEL CANVAS

Figure 1. Nine boxes on one page show how a venture makes money.

HOW IT IS USED

Print the canvas large, start with customer segments and value propositions, then fill the other blocks with sticky notes. Mark each note as tested or untested and update the canvas after every experiment.

WHEN IT IS USED

From idea stage to scale-up, especially when designing a new venture, comparing alternatives or planning a pivot.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Start-up founders, corporate innovation teams, consultants, investors and educators.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Vinted's canvas shows free listing for sellers as a value proposition, a buyer protection fee as the revenue stream and its own parcel network as a key resource.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier business-plan formats were text-heavy and static; the canvas made the business model visual, modular and easy to change (Osterwalder and Pigneur, 2010). It also made it possible to compare business models block by block.

Value Proposition Canvas

Designing products customers want by matching value map and customer profile

SLIBRARY 06 · SHEET 2 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

The Value Proposition Canvas is a tool that zooms into two blocks of the Business Model Canvas: a customer profile (jobs, pains and gains) and a value map (products and services, pain relievers and gain creators). Fit is achieved when the value map addresses the most important jobs, pains and gains of the customer (Osterwalder et al., 2014).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Alexander Osterwalder, Yves Pigneur, Gregory Bernarda and Alan Smith published the canvas in Value Proposition Design (Osterwalder et al., 2014), as a companion to the Business Model Canvas.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Teams often filled the value-proposition box with features they liked rather than with what customers needed. The canvas forces them to start from the customer's perspective.

FIGURE 1 · VALUE PROPOSITION CANVAS

Figure 1. Match what you offer to the jobs, pains and gains customers have.

HOW IT IS USED

First describe one customer segment: the jobs they try to get done, their pains and desired gains. Then list your products, pain relievers and gain creators, and check which pains and gains you really address.

WHEN IT IS USED

Before building an MVP and whenever customer feedback shows weak demand.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Product managers, designers, founders and marketing teams.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

For students selling clothes on Vinted, the job is 'clear my wardrobe and earn money', the pain is fees and effort, and free listing plus prepaid labels act as pain relievers.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

It connected customer research and design thinking directly to the business model (Osterwalder et al., 2014). It made 'fit' between offer and customer a visible, testable goal.

Lean Canvas

A one-page start-up plan built around problems, metrics and unfair advantage

SLIBRARY 06 · SHEET 3 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

The Lean Canvas is an adaptation of the Business Model Canvas for start-ups that replaces four blocks with problem, solution, key metrics and unfair advantage, keeping the focus on the riskiest assumptions of a new venture. It is designed to be completed quickly and updated as the founder learns (Maurya, 2012).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Ash Maurya, an entrepreneur and founder of LEANSTACK, adapted the Business Model Canvas for early-stage founders and described it in Running Lean (Maurya, 2012; 2022).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Maurya felt that the original canvas suited established businesses better than start-ups facing high uncertainty. He wanted a tool that pushed founders to start with the customer's problem.

FIGURE 1 · LEAN CANVAS

Figure 1. A start-up's business model, starting from the problem.

HOW IT IS USED

Fill in problem and customer segments first, then unique value proposition, solution, channels, revenue and costs, key metrics and unfair advantage. Rank the riskiest boxes and test them first.

WHEN IT IS USED

At idea stage and pre-MVP, and when comparing several start-up ideas quickly.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Early-stage founders, hackathon teams and start-up programmes.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A hackathon team writes 'Erasmus students cannot find a short-term flat before arrival' as the problem and 'number of verified bookings per week' as the key metric.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

It shifted the canvas from describing a business to testing a start-up, adding metrics and unfair advantage (Maurya, 2012). It made problem validation the first step.

Testing Business Ideas — Desirability, Feasibility, Viability

Three lenses to find and test the riskiest assumption first

SLIBRARY 06 · SHEET 4 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Testing Business Ideas is an experimentation approach that sorts business hypotheses into three risk types (desirability, feasibility and viability) and matches each with fast, cheap experiments, starting with the riskiest assumption. The aim is to reduce the risk of a new idea step by step before investing heavily (Bland and Osterwalder, 2019).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

David J. Bland, founder of the consultancy Precoil, wrote the field guide with Alexander Osterwalder (Bland and Osterwalder, 2019). The three lenses were popularised by the design consultancy IDEO.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Teams knew they should experiment but lacked a structured way to decide what to test first and which experiment to choose.

FIGURE 1 · TESTING BUSINESS IDEAS — DESIRABILITY, FEASIBILITY, VIABILITY

Figure 1. Do they want it, can we build it, should we?

HOW IT IS USED

Write hypotheses for each canvas block and label them D, F or V. Map them by importance and evidence, pick the most important assumption with the least evidence and choose a matching experiment, for example a landing page for desirability.

WHEN IT IS USED

Before and after building an MVP and before every major investment decision.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, innovation teams, product managers and corporate venture builders.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A team building an AI study-planner tests desirability with a fake-door landing page, feasibility by checking that user data stays protected and viability by asking students to pre-pay 3 euros.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

It linked the canvas tools to a library of practical experiments and evidence strength (Bland and Osterwalder, 2019). Testing became a planned, repeatable process rather than an occasional activity.

SWOT/TOWS

From a list of factors to four sets of strategic options

SLIBRARY 07 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

The TOWS matrix extends SWOT analysis by systematically combining external opportunities and threats with internal strengths and weaknesses to generate four types of strategy: SO (maxi-maxi), ST (maxi-mini), WO (mini-maxi) and WT (mini-mini). It turns a descriptive list into strategic options (Weihrich, 1982).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Heinz Weihrich, a management professor at the University of San Francisco, introduced the TOWS matrix in Long Range Planning (Weihrich, 1982), building on the older SWOT list used in strategic planning.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

SWOT analyses often ended as four lists without any decision. Weihrich wanted a tool that forces managers to connect internal and external factors and derive strategies.

FIGURE 1 · SWOT/TOWS

Figure 1. Match strengths and weaknesses to opportunities and threats to find moves.

HOW IT IS USED

List strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats, then fill four cells: use strengths to seize opportunities (SO), use strengths to counter threats (ST), fix weaknesses by using opportunities (WO) and minimise weaknesses and avoid threats (WT). Prioritise two or three strategies.

WHEN IT IS USED

When choosing a strategy for launch, entering a new market or reacting to a major change.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, strategy teams, consultants and students preparing business plans.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A Munich ride-sharing start-up combines its strength (strong student community) with an opportunity (rising public transport prices) into an SO strategy: campus-to-campus shared rides.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Weihrich (1982) added a matching step that the classic SWOT list lacked, turning analysis into strategy generation. It made the link between environment and internal capabilities explicit.

Porter's Five Forces

Analysing industry attractiveness before entering a digital market

SLIBRARY 07 · SHEET 2 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Porter's Five Forces is a framework for analysing industry structure through five competitive forces: rivalry among existing competitors, the threat of new entrants, the bargaining power of buyers, the bargaining power of suppliers and the threat of substitutes. Together these forces determine the profit potential of an industry (Porter, 2008).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Michael E. Porter of Harvard Business School introduced the framework in 1979 and refined it in 2008 (Porter, 1979; 2008), drawing on industrial organisation economics.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Managers focused narrowly on direct rivals. Porter showed that customers, suppliers, new entrants and substitutes also compete for an industry's profits.

FIGURE 1 · PORTER'S FIVE FORCES

Figure 1. Five pressures decide how much profit an industry leaves you.

HOW IT IS USED

Define the industry, then rate each force as low, medium or high and explain why. Identify which force limits profits most and design a strategy to reduce it.

WHEN IT IS USED

When deciding whether to enter a market, choosing a niche or explaining why profits are low.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Strategists, founders, investors and analysts.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

For second-hand fashion apps, buyer power is high because switching between apps is easy, which explains why Vinted invests in trust and convenience to keep users.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Porter (1979) shifted attention from competitors alone to the structure of the whole industry. Later work applied the forces to digital markets, where platforms can change buyer and supplier power (Porter, 2008).

Ansoff Matrix

Choosing a growth direction and understanding its level of risk

SLIBRARY 07 · SHEET 3 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

The Ansoff Matrix, or product–market growth matrix, sets out four growth strategies by combining existing or new products with existing or new markets: market penetration, market development, product development and diversification. Risk rises as a firm moves away from its current products and markets (Ansoff, 1957).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

H. Igor Ansoff, a mathematician and business manager often called a father of strategic management, presented the matrix in Harvard Business Review (Ansoff, 1957).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Firms looking to grow needed a simple way to compare growth options and their risk, especially when considering diversification.

FIGURE 1 · ANSOFF MATRIX

Figure 1. Four ways to grow: same or new products, same or new markets.

HOW IT IS USED

Place current and possible future offers in the four cells. Evaluate each option for risk, resources needed and fit, and choose one main direction for the next period.

WHEN IT IS USED

After first traction, when a venture decides how to grow next.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, marketing managers and corporate strategists.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Vinted used market development by launching in new European countries and product development by adding categories such as electronics (Vinted, 2025).

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Ansoff (1957) was among the first to give growth strategy a clear structure with explicit risk levels. It made diversification a deliberate choice rather than an opportunistic move.

Blue Ocean ERRC Grid

Redesigning the value curve to create uncontested market space

SLIBRARY 07 · SHEET 4 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

The ERRC Grid is a tool from Blue Ocean Strategy that asks which industry factors should be eliminated, reduced, raised and created to deliver a leap in value for buyers at lower cost. It supports value innovation, the pursuit of differentiation and low cost at the same time (Kim and Mauborgne, 2005).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne, professors at INSEAD, developed the grid as part of Blue Ocean Strategy (Kim and Mauborgne, 2005).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Most strategy tools helped firms compete in existing markets. Kim and Mauborgne wanted a way to create new market space where competition becomes irrelevant.

FIGURE 1 · BLUE OCEAN ERRC GRID

Figure 1. Eliminate, reduce, raise, create: redesign value, escape the red ocean.

HOW IT IS USED

List the factors your industry competes on. Decide which to eliminate, reduce, raise and create, then draw your new value curve against competitors.

WHEN IT IS USED

When a market is crowded, prices are falling or a founder looks for a distinctive positioning.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, product strategists and marketing teams.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Vinted eliminated seller fees, reduced listing effort with a simple app, raised trust with buyer protection and created its own pick-up network with Vinted Go.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

It rejected the idea that firms must choose between differentiation and low cost (Kim and Mauborgne, 2005). The grid gave managers a concrete action tool for value innovation.

VRIO / Resource-Based View

Testing which resources can create a lasting competitive advantage

SLIBRARY 08 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

The resource-based view argues that sustained competitive advantage comes from firm resources that are valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable and non-substitutable. The VRIO framework turns this into four questions about value, rarity, imitability and organisation (Barney, 1991; 1995).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Jay B. Barney, a strategic management scholar, set out the resource-based view in the Journal of Management (Barney, 1991) and later the VRIO questions (Barney, 1995).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Industry analysis explained why some markets are profitable but not why some firms in the same market outperform others. Barney looked inside the firm for the answer.

FIGURE 1 · VRIO / RESOURCE-BASED VIEW

Figure 1. Valuable, rare, hard to copy and organised: real advantage.

HOW IT IS USED

List your key resources and capabilities. For each ask: is it valuable, rare, costly to imitate and is the firm organised to exploit it? Only resources with four yeses give sustained advantage.

WHEN IT IS USED

When deciding which resources to build, buy or partner for, and when assessing competitive advantage for investors.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, strategists, investors and consultants.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A recommendation algorithm may be valuable but easy to copy, while Vinted's network of pick-up points and its user base are rare and costly to imitate.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Barney (1991) shifted strategy from the external market to internal resources. VRIO made the theory practical for managers (Barney, 1995).

Bullseye Traction Framework

Finding the one marketing channel that moves your start-up forward

SLIBRARY 08 · SHEET 2 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

The Bullseye Framework is a three-step process for finding the customer acquisition channel that will bring a start-up its next stage of growth: brainstorm ideas for all 19 traction channels, run cheap tests on the most promising, and focus resources on the channel that works best. It treats traction as a discipline equal to product development (Weinberg and Mares, 2015).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Gabriel Weinberg, founder of the search engine DuckDuckGo, and entrepreneur Justin Mares developed the framework from interviews with successful founders (Weinberg and Mares, 2015).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Founders tended to use only the channels they already knew, such as social media, and spread small budgets too thinly.

FIGURE 1 · BULLSEYE TRACTION FRAMEWORK

Figure 1. Brainstorm 19 channels, test three, focus on one.

HOW IT IS USED

Write one realistic idea for every channel (outer ring), choose the three most promising and test each cheaply (middle ring), then concentrate on the best performer (inner ring). Repeat when results fade.

WHEN IT IS USED

Right after MVP validation and whenever growth stalls.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, growth marketers and start-up teams.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A study-notes app tests TikTok content, university society partnerships and SEO; partnerships bring users at a quarter of the cost, so the team focuses there.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

It replaced 'try everything' or 'do what we know' with a structured test-and-focus process across 19 channels (Weinberg and Mares, 2015). Traction became part of start-up planning from day one.

Paid–Owned–Earned Media (POEM)

Planning a cross-media mix where channels reinforce each other

SLIBRARY 08 · SHEET 3 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Paid–Owned–Earned Media is a framework that classifies marketing channels by control and cost: paid media are bought (ads, sponsorships), owned media are controlled by the brand (website, app, newsletter, social accounts) and earned media are generated by others (reviews, word of mouth, press). Effective cross-media strategies combine all three (Corcoran, 2009).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Forrester analyst Sean Corcoran published the definitions that made the three terms standard industry language (Corcoran, 2009); academic research has since measured their effects (Lovett and Staelin, 2016).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

With the rise of social media, marketers needed a simple way to plan beyond paid advertising and to value word of mouth and their own channels.

FIGURE 1 · PAID–OWNED–EARNED MEDIA (POEM)

Figure 1. Buy attention, own the channel, earn the conversation.

HOW IT IS USED

List your current and possible channels under paid, owned and earned. Define the role of each (attract, convert, retain, amplify) and plan how paid activity will feed owned channels and generate earned media.

WHEN IT IS USED

When planning a launch campaign and setting a marketing budget.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Marketers, founders and communication agencies.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Vinted combines paid TV ads, its owned app and newsletter, and earned media from users sharing their finds and from branded series coverage (Gollasch, 2025).

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

It moved planning from media buying to an integrated view of all touchpoints (Corcoran, 2009). Research shows that the three types interact rather than act alone (Lovett and Staelin, 2016).

Unit Economics: LTV/CAC

Checking whether each new customer creates or destroys value

SLIBRARY 08 · SHEET 4 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Unit economics compares the lifetime value of a customer, the present value of the margins a customer generates over the relationship, with the cost of acquiring that customer. A venture grows profitably only when lifetime value clearly exceeds acquisition cost (Gupta, Lehmann and Stuart, 2004).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Sunil Gupta and Donald R. Lehmann, marketing professors at Columbia Business School at the time, showed how customer lifetime value can be used to value whole firms (Gupta, Lehmann and Stuart, 2004; Gupta and Lehmann, 2005).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Fast-growing internet firms reported user numbers without showing whether those users were profitable. Customer lifetime value offered a way to link marketing to firm value.

FIGURE 1 · UNIT ECONOMICS: LTV/CAC

Figure 1. A customer must be worth more than it costs to win.

HOW IT IS USED

Estimate average margin per customer per period, retention rate and a discount rate to calculate LTV. Divide total marketing and sales spend by new customers to get CAC, and compare the two per channel.

WHEN IT IS USED

Before scaling marketing spend, in budgeting and when preparing for investors.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, CFOs, growth teams and investors.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

If a marketplace pays €12 to acquire a buyer who generates €3 of fees per year and stays four years, the LTV of about €12 only just covers the cost.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

It treated customers as assets whose value can be estimated (Gupta and Lehmann, 2005). Later work applied the same logic to valuing non-subscription firms from public data (McCarthy and Fader, 2018).

Stakeholder Salience Model

Prioritising stakeholders by power, legitimacy and urgency

SLIBRARY 09 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A model that identifies and prioritises stakeholders according to three attributes: power to influence the firm, legitimacy of their relationship or claim, and urgency of their claim. The more attributes a stakeholder holds, the more salient, or important, they are to managers (Mitchell, Agle and Wood, 1997).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Ronald K. Mitchell, Bradley R. Agle and Donna J. Wood developed the model in a 1997 article in the Academy of Management Review (Mitchell, Agle and Wood, 1997).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Stakeholder theory said firms should consider everyone affected by them, but gave managers little help in deciding who to listen to first. The model offered a systematic way to answer the question of who and what really counts.

FIGURE 1 · STAKEHOLDER SALIENCE MODEL

Figure 1. Power, legitimacy and urgency decide whose voice counts first.

HOW IT IS USED

List all stakeholders, then score each on power, legitimacy and urgency. Place them in a three-circle Venn diagram; those with all three (definitive stakeholders) need immediate attention, while those with one attribute can be monitored.

WHEN IT IS USED

At founding, before fundraising and whenever the venture enters a new stage or market, because stakeholders gain and lose attributes over time.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, product managers, investor-relations and public-affairs teams.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

For Langfuse in 2023, Lightspeed (power, legitimacy) became definitive when the seed round was urgent; open-source users had legitimacy and urgency but little formal power until they became paying customers.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier stakeholder maps treated stakeholders as static groups. Mitchell, Agle and Wood (1997) added urgency and showed that salience is dynamic, so a latent stakeholder can suddenly become definitive.

Power–Interest Grid

Four stakeholder strategies from two simple questions

SLIBRARY 09 · SHEET 2 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A two-by-two matrix that classifies stakeholders by their power over an organisation and their interest in its decisions, giving four strategies: manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed and monitor. It originates in Mendelow's work on environmental scanning and was popularised in strategy textbooks (Mendelow, 1981; Johnson, Scholes and Whittington, 2008).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Aubrey L. Mendelow proposed classifying stakeholders by power and interest in a 1981 conference paper on environmental scanning; the grid was later popularised by Johnson and Scholes in their strategy textbook (Mendelow, 1981; Johnson, Scholes and Whittington, 2008).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Managers needed a quick, visual way to decide how much attention and communication each stakeholder group deserved. The grid turns a long stakeholder list into four clear action strategies.

FIGURE 1 · POWER–INTEREST GRID

Figure 1. Map who can block you and who cares — then act accordingly.

HOW IT IS USED

List stakeholders, rate each one's power (ability to influence) and interest (how much they care) as high or low, and place them on the grid. Then plan communication: manage high-power/high-interest players closely and keep high-power/low-interest players satisfied.

WHEN IT IS USED

Before key decisions such as a pivot, a funding round, a product launch or entering a regulated market.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, project managers, consultants and students preparing business plans.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A Munich food-delivery start-up might place the city council (high power, low interest) in 'keep satisfied' and couriers (high interest, growing power) in 'manage closely'.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier stakeholder lists did not translate into actions. Mendelow's grid linked each position to a concrete engagement strategy, which made stakeholder analysis practical for managers (Johnson, Scholes and Whittington, 2008).

Incubators vs Accelerators

Choosing the right support model for your venture's stage

SLIBRARY 09 · SHEET 4 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A distinction between business incubators, which offer open-ended support such as space, shared services and advice, and seed accelerators, which run fixed-term, cohort-based programmes with mentoring and education that end in a Demo Day, usually in exchange for equity (Cohen and Hochberg, 2014).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Susan Cohen (University of Richmond) and Yael Hochberg (Rice University) defined the seed accelerator and contrasted it with incubators and angel investors (Cohen, 2013; Cohen and Hochberg, 2014).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

After Y Combinator (2005) and Techstars (2006), hundreds of programmes called themselves accelerators or incubators without clear meaning. Researchers needed a precise definition to study whether these programmes actually help start-ups.

FIGURE 1 · INCUBATORS VS ACCELERATORS

Figure 1. Incubators give shelter and time; accelerators give speed and a cohort.

HOW IT IS USED

Compare programmes on duration, cohort structure, investment and equity, education and mentoring, and the end point (Demo Day or not). Match the model to your bottleneck: time and space, or speed and network.

WHEN IT IS USED

Early stage: incubators before or around the first prototype; accelerators once there is a team and something testable.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, universities, corporates designing programmes and investors.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Langfuse used Y Combinator's three-month batch to test several ideas quickly; a Munich team still building its first paper prototype might be better served first by an incubator such as WERK1.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Classic incubators were property-based and long-term. Cohen and Hochberg (2014) showed that accelerators were a new model built on limited duration, cohorts and intensive mentoring that compresses learning.

Discover, evaluate, exploit: the anatomy of an opportunity

SLIBRARY 10 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A view of entrepreneurship as the meeting of enterprising individuals and valuable opportunities, studied through how opportunities are discovered, evaluated and exploited, by whom and with what effects (Shane and Venkataraman, 2000).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Scott Shane and S. Venkataraman set out the nexus in a highly cited Academy of Management Review article (Shane and Venkataraman, 2000).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Entrepreneurship research had focused on the traits of entrepreneurs and struggled to define its own field. The nexus shifted attention to opportunities and to why some people, and not others, spot and act on them.

FIGURE 1 · INDIVIDUAL–OPPORTUNITY NEXUS

Figure 1. Entrepreneurship happens where an alert person meets a real opportunity.

Describe the opportunity (what is changing?), the individual (what prior knowledge and network do you have?) and the fit between them. Then move through discovery, evaluation and exploitation, deciding at each step whether to continue.

WHEN IT IS USED

In the earliest phase, when scanning ideas and deciding which one fits the team.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, idea-stage teams, researchers and corporate innovation units.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Langfuse's founders had engineering and fintech experience and sat among AI teams at YC; their prior knowledge let them discover the LLM-monitoring opportunity others overlooked.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Trait research asked 'who is an entrepreneur?'. Shane and Venkataraman (2000) asked how opportunities and individuals combine, making the process, not the personality, the unit of analysis.

Double Diamond

From a fuzzy problem to a tested concept in four phases

SLIBRARY 10 · SHEET 2 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A design process model in four phases, Discover, Define, Develop and Deliver, drawn as two diamonds in which teams first explore widely (divergent thinking) and then focus (convergent thinking), once for the problem and once for the solution (Design Council, 2019).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

The UK Design Council introduced the Double Diamond in 2004 after studying design processes in leading companies and expanded it into its Framework for Innovation in 2019 (Design Council, 2019).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Designers and managers lacked a simple shared picture of the creative process. The model shows that good solutions require time spent understanding the problem before generating ideas.

FIGURE 1 · DOUBLE DIAMOND

Figure 1. Diverge, converge — twice: first on the problem, then on the solution.

HOW IT IS USED

Discover: research users widely. Define: narrow insights into one clear problem statement. Develop: generate and prototype many solutions. Deliver: test, select and launch the best one.

WHEN IT IS USED

When translating a selected opportunity into a product or service concept, and whenever a team jumps to solutions too early.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Design teams, product managers, public-sector innovators and student project teams.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Langfuse's founders explored several AI app ideas (Discover), defined the shared problem of LLM observability (Define), and then built and launched an open-source tool (Develop, Deliver).

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Linear stage models treated design as a straight path. The Double Diamond made divergence and convergence visible and placed problem definition on equal footing with solution design (Design Council, 2019).

Understand the progress customers want, not just who they are

SLIBRARY 10 · SHEET 3 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A theory stating that customers 'hire' products and services to make progress in a specific circumstance, so innovators should understand the functional, social and emotional job to be done rather than customer demographics (Christensen et al., 2016).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Clayton M. Christensen of Harvard Business School, with Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon and David S. Duncan, set out the theory in Harvard Business Review and the book Competing Against Luck (Christensen et al., 2016).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Most new products fail because companies segment by customer attributes and miss why people actually buy. The theory gives a causal explanation of purchase decisions.

FIGURE 1 · JOBS-TO-BE-DONE

Figure 1. Customers do not buy products; they hire them to make progress.

HOW IT IS USED

Interview customers about the last time they switched to a solution, identify the circumstance and the progress they wanted, and list the functional, social and emotional dimensions. Design the concept around that job.

WHEN IT IS USED

When translating an idea into a product concept, and when deciding which features matter.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Product managers, founders, marketers and UX researchers.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Developers hired Langfuse to answer one job: 'help me see why my AI app gave a bad answer, so I can fix it before customers notice'.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Traditional segmentation grouped customers by age, income or product category. Christensen et al. (2016) shifted focus to the circumstance and the causal job, explaining why very different products compete for the same job.

Profiting from Innovation

Why inventors lose and imitators win — and how to avoid it

SLIBRARY 10 · SHEET 4 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A framework explaining why innovators often fail to profit from their innovations: profits depend on the appropriability regime (how well the innovation can be protected, for example by patents or secrecy), the dominant design and access to complementary assets such as distribution, brand or manufacturing (Teece, 1986).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

David J. Teece, an economist at the University of California, Berkeley, published the framework in Research Policy (Teece, 1986).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Many pioneering firms, such as early makers of CT scanners, lost markets to followers. Teece explained the pattern and gave managers a way to decide whether to protect, partner or integrate.

FIGURE 1 · PROFITING FROM INNOVATION

Figure 1. Inventing is not enough — capture value with protection or complementary assets.

HOW IT IS USED

Assess whether your IP regime is tight (patentable, hard to copy) or weak, then identify which complementary assets you need and whether they are generic or specialised. If protection is weak, build or secure complementary assets fast, or partner with those who hold them.

WHEN IT IS USED

When deciding whether to file patents, open-source a product, partner or sell the company.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, IP lawyers, investors and corporate strategists.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Software is hard to patent in Europe 'as such', so Langfuse open-sourced its code and built complementary assets — a developer community, a managed cloud and enterprise trust — which ClickHouse then acquired.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier thinking assumed the first mover with the best technology wins. Teece (1986) showed that weak appropriability and complementary assets often decide who profits, a view he later extended to digital platforms.

Map, sketch, decide, prototype, test — in one week

SLIBRARY 11 · SHEET 1 / 4

Teams were spending months building products before learning whether customers wanted them. The sprint compresses design thinking, prototyping and testing into one week so teams can learn without building and launching.

FIGURE 1 · DESIGN SPRINT

Figure 1. Five days from big problem to tested prototype with real customers.

HOW IT IS USED

Monday: map the problem and choose a target. Tuesday: sketch solutions individually. Wednesday: decide and storyboard. Thursday: build a realistic façade prototype. Friday: test with five target customers and record patterns.

WHEN IT IS USED

Before committing engineering resources to a new product, feature or pivot.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Start-up teams, corporate innovation units, product designers and student project groups.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A Munich student team planning a study-room booking app could build a Figma façade on Thursday and test it with five students in the library on Friday.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Design thinking processes were open-ended and often took weeks. The Design Sprint added a strict timebox, a decider role and same-week customer testing (Knapp, Zeratsky and Kowitz, 2016).

Throughout early product development, especially when choosing what to build next.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

UX designers, product teams, engineers and founders.

Kano Model

Sorting features into must-haves, performance and delighters

SLIBRARY 11 · SHEET 3 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A model that classifies product attributes by how they affect customer satisfaction: must-be qualities cause dissatisfaction when missing, one-dimensional qualities increase satisfaction proportionally, and attractive qualities delight when present but are not missed when absent (Kano et al., 1984).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Noriaki Kano, a quality-management professor at Tokyo University of Science, developed the model with Nobuhiko Seraku, Fumio Takahashi and Shinichi Tsuji (Kano et al., 1984).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Traditional quality thinking assumed that more of any feature always increases satisfaction. Kano showed that the relationship differs by feature type, which helps teams prioritise.

FIGURE 1 · KANO MODEL

Figure 1. Some features are expected, some scale satisfaction, some delight.

HOW IT IS USED

For each feature ask users two questions: how they would feel if it were present and if it were absent. Classify answers as must-be, one-dimensional, attractive, indifferent or reverse, then prioritise must-be first.

WHEN IT IS USED

When deciding the feature set of a prototype or MVP, and when reviewing features after launch.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Product managers, UX researchers and quality managers.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

For a banking app, secure login is must-be, fast transfers are one-dimensional, and instant spending insights may be attractive; the AI Pin's laser projector impressed in demos but frustrated in daily use.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier quality models treated satisfaction as one linear scale. Kano et al. (1984) introduced non-linear categories and showed that delighters become expected over time.

Six dimensions to compare how cultures shape business behaviour

SLIBRARY 13 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A model that compares national cultures along six dimensions: power distance, individualism versus collectivism, masculinity versus femininity, uncertainty avoidance, long-term versus short-term orientation, and indulgence versus restraint. WHO DEVELOPED IT

Dutch social psychologist Geert Hofstede (1928–2020) developed the model from employee attitude surveys at IBM subsidiaries in dozens of countries (Hofstede, 1980). Later editions added long-term orientation and indulgence, the latter with Michael Minkov (Hofstede, Hofstede and Minkov, 2010).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Multinational managers needed a systematic way to understand why the same policies worked differently in different countries. Hofstede offered measurable country scores instead of anecdotes and stereotypes.

FIGURE 1 · HOFSTEDE'S CULTURAL DIMENSIONS

Figure 1. National culture shapes how people lead, buy, risk and start.

HOW IT IS USED

Look up the scores of your home market and target market, compare the biggest gaps and ask what each gap means for your product, pricing, marketing and team management. Validate the assumptions with local customers and partners.

WHEN IT IS USED

Before entering a new country, building an international team or localising a digital product's messaging and onboarding.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, international marketers, HR managers and researchers comparing countries.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A German fintech entering Nigeria might adapt its onboarding for higher power distance and collectivism, for example by using trusted community agents and family referral rewards.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier cross-cultural work was largely descriptive and qualitative; Hofstede quantified culture with comparable country scores on independent dimensions (Hofstede, 1980). This made culture usable as a variable in management research and strategy.

SLIBRARY 13 · SHEET 2 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A framework stating that institutions rest on three pillars: regulative (laws, rules and sanctions), normative (values, norms and professional expectations) and cultural-cognitive (shared beliefs and taken-for-granted ways of seeing). WHO DEVELOPED IT

American sociologist W. Richard Scott of Stanford University synthesised decades of institutional theory into the three-pillar model (Scott, 1995). It has been refined across four editions of his book (Scott, 2014).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Institutional theory used 'institutions' in many inconsistent ways. Scott separated the different mechanisms — coercion, obligation and shared understanding — so researchers and managers could analyse them systematically.

Figure 1. Rules, norms and beliefs: the three walls around every venture.

List the laws and regulations affecting your venture (regulative), the expectations of professionals and society (normative) and what customers take for granted (cultural-cognitive). Mark each force as an enabler or barrier and decide whether to comply, adapt or try to change it.

WHEN IT IS USED

When assessing a new market, a regulated sector such as fintech or health, or when a venture faces legitimacy problems.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, policy makers, ecosystem builders and strategy researchers.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Jumia's low card use in many markets reflects cultural-cognitive habits and regulative payment rules, which is why it built its own JumiaPay and cash-on-delivery options.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier institutional theories often emphasised only rules or only culture; Scott's model integrated three distinct pillars in one analytical frame (Scott, 1995). This lets founders see that changing a law does not automatically change habits or beliefs.

WHEN IT IS USED

Before founding, during team formation, and in entrepreneurship education and incubation programmes.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Educators, incubators, mentors and aspiring founders assessing readiness.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A student who runs a two-week pre-sales test for a campus app gains a mastery experience that raises confidence to pitch for the Digital Venture Project.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

SLIBRARY 14 · SHEET 3 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard first published the 'life cycle theory of leadership' in 1969 (Hersey and Blanchard, 1969). It was renamed Situational Leadership in their textbook Management of Organizational Behavior (Hersey and Blanchard, 1977).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Trait and 'one best style' theories did not explain why the same leader succeeded with one team and failed with another. The model gave managers a practical way to diagnose followers and adapt.

FIGURE 1 · SITUATIONAL LEADERSHIP

Figure 1. Lead the person in front of you, not a fixed style.

HOW IT IS USED

For each team member and task, assess competence and commitment, choose the matching style (tell, sell, participate, delegate) and review as readiness grows.

WHEN IT IS USED

When a start-up hires its first employees, onboards interns or volunteers, or when founders hand over control as the venture grows.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, team leads, project managers and student group leaders.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

After Jumia's founders stepped down, a new CEO had to give clear direction during the cost reset (telling) while later delegating more decisions to country teams.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier models such as the Ohio State studies described task and relationship behaviour; Hersey and Blanchard (1969) linked them to follower maturity and made style a choice rather than a fixed trait. This connects leadership with self-reflection.

Growth Mindset

How beliefs about ability shape responses to challenge and failure

SLIBRARY 14 · SHEET 4 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A growth mindset is the belief that basic abilities and intelligence can be developed through effort, good strategies and help from others, whereas a fixed mindset sees them as unchangeable traits. The mindset a person holds influences how they respond to challenge, feedback and failure (Dweck, 2006).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Stanford psychologist Carol S. Dweck developed the theory from decades of research on motivation and achievement (Dweck, 2006).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Dweck wanted to understand why some students give up after failure while equally able students try harder. She found that beliefs about whether ability can change explain much of the difference.

FIGURE 1 · GROWTH MINDSET

Figure 1. Abilities grow with effort, strategy and feedback.

HOW IT IS USED

Notice fixed-mindset self-talk ('I'm not a tech person'), reframe it as a learning goal ('I can't code yet'), seek feedback and treat failed tests as data rather than verdicts.

WHEN IT IS USED

After rejection by investors or customers, when learning new digital skills, and when building a learning culture in an early team.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, educators, coaches and team leaders.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A founding team that treats a failed landing-page test as information about the message, not proof that 'we are bad at marketing', will iterate faster.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier views of talent emphasised innate ability; Dweck (2006) showed that beliefs about ability can themselves be changed and influence outcomes. This directly supports the idea that founders are made, not born.

Triple Bottom Line

Measuring business success on economic, environmental and social performance

SLIBRARY 15 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

An accounting and management concept proposing that businesses should measure and report their performance on three dimensions — economic prosperity, environmental quality and social justice — rather than on profit alone. It is often summarised as people, planet and profit (Elkington, 1997).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

British sustainability pioneer John Elkington coined the term in 1994 and developed it in his book Cannibals with Forks (Elkington, 1994; Elkington, 1997). He co-founded the consultancy SustainAbility and later Volans.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Companies reported only financial results, so environmental and social damage stayed invisible. Elkington wanted a simple way to make managers and investors pay attention to all three.

FIGURE 1 · TRIPLE BOTTOM LINE

Figure 1. People, planet, profit — measure all three, not just one.

HOW IT IS USED

Define two or three indicators for each bottom line (for example revenue, CO2e avoided, jobs or meals provided), measure them regularly and report them together. Use trade-offs between the three as a basis for decisions.

WHEN IT IS USED

When defining a venture's mission and KPIs, preparing impact reports or applying for B Corp certification.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, sustainability managers, impact investors and reporting teams.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Too Good To Go can report revenue (profit), CO2e avoided (planet) and affordable meals plus extra shop income (people) side by side.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Traditional accounting focused on a single financial bottom line; Elkington (1997) proposed equal attention to environmental and social performance. In 2018 he criticised its reduction to an accounting tool and called for a deeper system change (Elkington, 2018).

SLIBRARY 15 · SHEET 2 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A business model design tool that extends the Business Model Canvas with an environmental layer based on life-cycle perspective and a social layer based on stakeholder perspective. WHO DEVELOPED IT

Alexandre Joyce and Raymond L. Paquin, a management professor at Concordia University's John Molson School of Business, published the tool in the Journal of Cleaner Production (Joyce and Paquin, 2016).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

The original Business Model Canvas focused on economic value only. Founders who wanted sustainability needed a visual, practical tool that shows environmental and social effects in the same structure.

FIGURE 1 · TRIPLE-LAYERED BUSINESS MODEL CANVAS

Figure 1. Three stacked canvases: economic, environmental, social.

Fill in the economic canvas first, then the environmental layer (functional value, materials, production, supplies and outsourcing, distribution, use phase, end-of-life, impacts, benefits) and the social layer (social value, employees, governance, communities, societal culture, scale of outreach, end-users, impacts, benefits). Draw arrows where a change in one layer affects the others.

WHEN IT IS USED

When designing or redesigning a digital venture's model, preparing an impact pitch or comparing sustainable alternatives.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, product teams, sustainability consultants and students in venture projects.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

For Too Good To Go, the economic layer shows fees per bag, the environmental layer shows food and CO2e saved, and the social layer shows affordable meals and partner shop income.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

It builds on the Business Model Canvas (Osterwalder and Pigneur, 2010) but adds life-cycle and stakeholder layers with vertical coherence. This lets designers see trade-offs across layers instead of treating sustainability separately (Joyce and Paquin, 2016).

Circular Economy Butterfly

Technical and biological loops that design out waste and pollution

SLIBRARY 15 · SHEET 3 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A system diagram of the circular economy showing two cycles: a technical cycle in which products and materials are kept in use through sharing, maintenance, reuse, remanufacturing and recycling, and a biological cycle in which biodegradable materials are returned safely to nature. Inner loops retain more value than outer ones (Ellen MacArthur Foundation, 2013).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

The Ellen MacArthur Foundation, founded by record-breaking sailor Dame Ellen MacArthur in 2010, published the butterfly diagram in its 2013 report (Ellen MacArthur Foundation, 2013). It draws on cradle-to-cradle design (McDonough and Braungart, 2002).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

The linear 'take-make-waste' economy wastes resources and creates pollution. The foundation wanted a clear economic and business case for an alternative system.

FIGURE 1 · CIRCULAR ECONOMY BUTTERFLY

Figure 1. Two loops — technical and biological — keep materials in use.

HOW IT IS USED

Locate your product or service in the diagram, identify where value is currently lost, and redesign to move activities into tighter inner loops (share, maintain, reuse) before outer loops (recycle). For food and other biological materials, plan cascades and safe return to soil.

WHEN IT IS USED

During product design, choice of materials, supply-chain design and when developing circular revenue models such as resale or repair.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Designers, founders, supply-chain managers and policy makers.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Vinted operates in the inner technical loop by enabling clothing reuse, while Too Good To Go operates in the biological cycle by keeping edible food in use for people.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Recycling-focused approaches looked at waste at the end; the butterfly shows a hierarchy of loops and distinguishes technical from biological materials (Ellen MacArthur Foundation, 2013). It reframed circularity as a source of business opportunity.

Technology Acceptance Model

Why users accept or resist a new digital tool

SLIBRARY 16 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A model explaining that a person's intention to use a new information technology depends mainly on two beliefs: perceived usefulness, the degree to which they believe the system would enhance their job performance, and perceived ease of use, the degree to which they believe using it would be free from effort (Davis, 1989).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Fred D. Davis developed the model in his doctoral work at MIT's Sloan School of Management (Davis, 1986) and published the validated scales in MIS Quarterly (Davis, 1989).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Companies invested heavily in information systems that employees did not use. Davis wanted a simple, measurable model to predict and improve adoption.

FIGURE 1 · TECHNOLOGY ACCEPTANCE MODEL

Figure 1. Useful and easy to use — or it will not be used.

HOW IT IS USED

Survey or interview target users about usefulness and ease of use, identify the weaker belief, and redesign the product, onboarding or communication to strengthen it. Re-test after changes.

WHEN IT IS USED

Before and after launching a digital tool to partners or customers, especially traditional businesses that are new to digital processes.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Product managers, UX researchers, founders and digital transformation consultants.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A local bakery will join Too Good To Go only if listing surplus bags is clearly useful (extra income, less waste) and takes seconds at closing time.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

It simplified broader attitude theories into two technology-specific beliefs with validated scales (Davis, 1989). Later extensions such as TAM2 and UTAUT added social influence and facilitating conditions (Venkatesh and Davis, 2000; Venkatesh et al., 2003).

SLIBRARY 16 · SHEET 2 / 4

A model describing two broad functions that mentors provide: career functions (sponsorship, exposure and visibility, coaching, protection and challenging assignments) and psychosocial functions (role modelling, acceptance and confirmation, counselling and friendship) (Kram, 1985).

Organisational behaviour scholar Kathy E. Kram developed the model from in-depth studies of mentor relationships (Kram, 1983; Kram, 1985). She later showed that peers can also provide these functions (Kram and Isabella, 1985).

Mentoring was widely praised but poorly understood. Kram identified what mentors actually do and how relationships develop over time.

Figure 1. Mentors help your career and support you as a person.

List the support you need, sort it into career and psychosocial functions, and map who in your network provides each. Build a 'developmental network' of several mentors and peers instead of relying on one person.

WHEN IT IS USED

From idea stage onwards, especially in incubators, accelerators, founder communities and after setbacks.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, incubator and accelerator managers, mentors and HR professionals.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A student founder might get psychosocial support from a peer founder circle (moral support) and career support from an accelerator mentor who introduces investors (professional support).

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier views saw mentoring as informal sponsorship by a senior manager; Kram (1985) distinguished career and psychosocial functions and phases of the relationship. This links neatly to the moral-support and professional-support networks of digital entrepreneurs.

SLIBRARY 16 · SHEET 3 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A four-stage model of learning in which concrete experience leads to reflective observation, abstract conceptualisation and active experimentation, which in turn creates new experience. Learning is the process whereby knowledge is created through the transformation of experience (Kolb, 1984).

American educational theorist David A. Kolb developed the model, drawing on the work of Dewey, Lewin and Piaget (Kolb, 1984).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Traditional education focused on transmitting abstract knowledge. Kolb wanted to explain how adults actually learn from experience at work and in life.

Figure 1. Do, reflect, think, try — then repeat for life.

HOW IT IS USED

After each venture experiment, write down what happened (experience), what you noticed (reflection), what general lesson or concept it suggests (conceptualisation) and what you will try next (experimentation). Keep the cycle running as a habit.

WHEN IT IS USED

Continuously: after customer interviews, launches, failures and when learning new digital skills.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, educators, coaches and anyone building lifelong learning habits.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

After a failed pop-up test, a sustainable fashion start-up reflects on low sign-ups, links them to the Technology Acceptance Model, and tests a simpler onboarding flow.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

It made experience and reflection central to learning instead of treating them as side effects (Kolb, 1984). It also underpins research on how entrepreneurs transform experience into knowledge (Politis, 2005).

When defining a venture's value proposition, partnerships and impact metrics.

Founders, strategists, corporate leaders and impact investors.

Financial Growth Cycle

Which investors and lenders fit each stage of a growing venture

SLIBRARY 17 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

The financial growth cycle explains how a firm's mix of finance changes as it grows: very young, informationally opaque firms rely on insiders, friends and business angels, while older firms with track records can access venture capital, bank debt and finally public markets. Size, age and information availability decide which sources are realistic (Berger and Udell, 1998).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Allen N. Berger and Gregory F. Udell developed the model in a widely cited 1998 article on small business finance, written when Berger was an economist at the US Federal Reserve Board (Berger and Udell, 1998).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Earlier finance theory focused on large listed companies. Berger and Udell wanted to explain why small firms face different options and why information gaps make some sources unavailable at the start.

FIGURE 1 · FINANCIAL GROWTH CYCLE

Figure 1. Different money fits different stages — match the source to your size.

HOW IT IS USED

Place your venture on the cycle by age, size and evidence available (revenue, data, collateral). List the sources that typically serve that stage and check which ones you can realistically approach in the next 12 months.

WHEN IT IS USED

At the start of every fundraising plan and whenever the venture moves into a new stage, e.g. from first revenue to scaling.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, finance students, business angels, bank relationship managers and start-up advisers.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A Munich student app with no revenue relies on savings and an EXIST-style grant or angel money; once it shows recurring revenue it can approach seed VCs, and later a bank or venture-debt fund.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

It linked the choice of finance to information opacity and firm age instead of treating capital structure as a static decision. It also showed that private equity and debt markets complement each other over the life of a firm (Berger and Udell, 1998).

PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Pecking Order Theory

Why information gaps make some money cheaper than other money

SLIBRARY 17 · SHEET 3 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Pecking order theory states that firms prefer internal funds first, then debt, and issue new equity only as a last resort, because outside investors know less than managers and price new shares at a discount. The information gap makes each step down the order more expensive (Myers and Majluf, 1984; Myers, 1984).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Stewart C. Myers of MIT Sloan and Nicholas S. Majluf modelled the problem in 1984, and Myers named the 'pecking order' in his paper 'The capital structure puzzle' the same year (Myers, 1984).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Classic theory could not explain why profitable firms borrowed little and why share prices often fell when new equity was announced. Asymmetric information offered the explanation.

FIGURE 1 · PECKING ORDER THEORY

Figure 1. Use your own cash first, borrow second, sell shares last.

HOW IT IS USED

List all funding options from internal to external. For each, estimate its cost including dilution and control loss, and ask what signal it sends to investors.

WHEN IT IS USED

When a venture with some revenue decides how to fund the next step — reinvest profit, take a loan, or raise a round.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

CFOs, founders with revenue, investors interpreting funding announcements.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Vinted reached profit in 2023 and could fund growth from its own cash flow — a strong position compared with start-ups forced to sell equity under time pressure.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

It replaced the trade-off view of an optimal debt ratio with a hierarchy driven by information asymmetry. It explained financing behaviour by signalling rather than taxes alone (Myers and Majluf, 1984).

Draw your own cash curve from idea to break-even. Mark where grants end and where investors start to show interest, then plan bridge sources (grants, bootstrapping, pre-orders, debt) for the gap.

At pre-seed and seed stage, especially for deep-tech or hardware ventures with long development cycles.

Founders, public funders such as KfW or EXIST programmes, university transfer offices, policy makers.

Burn Rate, Runway & Fume Date

Three numbers every founder must know before talking to investors

SLIBRARY 18 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Burn rate is the cash a venture spends per month; net burn subtracts monthly cash income. Runway equals cash in the bank divided by net monthly burn, and the fume date is the calendar date on which cash runs out if nothing changes. A start-up is 'default alive' if, at current growth and costs, it reaches profitability before the fume date (Graham, 2015).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

The terms come from start-up practice rather than one author. Paul Graham, co-founder of Y Combinator, popularised the related 'default alive or default dead' test in a 2015 essay (Graham, 2015).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Many start-ups fail not because the idea is bad but because they run out of cash while raising. A simple shared language for burn and runway helps founders and boards act in time.

FIGURE 1 · BURN RATE, RUNWAY & FUME DATE

Figure 1. Know when your money runs out — and raise long before.

HOW IT IS USED

Net monthly burn = monthly cash out − monthly cash in. Runway (months) = cash ÷ net monthly burn. Fume date = today + runway. Start raising at fume date − time to clear (often 6–9 months for a round) − a safety buffer.

WHEN IT IS USED

Every month from the first euro raised; it is the core of every board meeting and pitch Q&A.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, CFOs, boards, investors and lenders checking financial health.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A team with €600,000 in the bank, €80,000 monthly costs and €20,000 monthly revenue has a net burn of €60,000 and a 10-month runway; with 6 months to close a round it must start fundraising within 4 months.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Graham's test turned a static runway figure into a forward-looking question about growth and costs. It forces founders to ask whether they can survive without the next round (Graham, 2015).

Build a simple cap table before and after the round. Mark each term as economics or control, and model what founders receive in an exit at several sale prices, including liquidation preferences.

WHEN IT IS USED

From the first angel or seed round onwards, and before any convertible note or SAFE.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, angels, VCs, start-up lawyers and student founders in pitch competitions.

A €1m seed round at €4m pre-money gives investors 20%; a 1x liquidation preference means they get their €1m back first if the company sells for little.

SLIBRARY 18 · SHEET 3 / 4

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Guy Kawasaki, former Apple evangelist and venture capitalist, published the rule on his blog in December 2005 (Kawasaki, 2005).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

As a VC, Kawasaki sat through many long, text-heavy pitches. He wanted a simple rule that made presentations shorter, clearer and easier to judge.

FIGURE 1 · PITCH DECK 10/20/30

Figure 1. Ten slides, twenty minutes, nothing smaller than thirty points.

Cover ten topics (problem, solution, business model, underlying magic, marketing and sales, competition, team, projections and milestones, status and timeline, summary and call to action). Rehearse to 20 minutes and cut any slide that needs small text.

WHEN IT IS USED

When preparing investor, accelerator or competition pitches.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, students in pitch simulations, accelerator coaches.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A Munich ed-tech team cuts a 28-slide deck to ten, moving detailed financials to an appendix and leading with traction from 300 paying students.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

It turned pitching advice into a memorable numeric constraint. It moved the emphasis from showing everything to showing the essential evidence (Kawasaki, 2015).

Bootstrapping strategies

Creative ways to fund a venture from customers, partners and savings

SLIBRARY 18 · SHEET 4 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Financial bootstrapping is the use of methods to secure resources without relying on long-term external finance from banks or investors. Winborg and Landström identified clusters of methods such as owner financing, minimising accounts receivable, sharing resources with other firms, delaying payments, minimising inventory and using subsidies (Winborg and Landström, 2001).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Swedish researchers Joakim Winborg and Hans Landström studied how small business managers acquire resources and published their survey-based typology in 2001 (Winborg and Landström, 2001).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Research focused on bank loans and venture capital, yet most small firms never use them. The authors wanted to capture the many informal ways entrepreneurs actually finance themselves.

FIGURE 1 · BOOTSTRAPPING STRATEGIES

Figure 1. Get resources without giving away shares.

HOW IT IS USED

List all costs for the next six months. For each, ask whether it can be shared, delayed, paid by the customer upfront, replaced by a grant or avoided. Track how many months of runway the measures add.

WHEN IT IS USED

At pre-seed, between funding rounds and whenever investors are cautious.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Solo founders, student start-ups, small digital agencies and SaaS founders who want to keep control.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A student SaaS start-up can ask for annual prepayment, use free cloud credits and share a co-working space to extend runway.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

It treated bootstrapping as a set of deliberate strategies rather than a sign of weakness. It linked financing to everyday management practices such as payment terms and shared offices (Winborg and Landström, 2001).

Three Ethical Theories

Outcomes, duties and character: three questions for every digital decision

SLIBRARY 19 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Normative ethical theories offer different tests for right action: consequentialism judges actions by their outcomes for everyone affected, deontology by whether they respect duties and rights regardless of outcomes, and virtue ethics by whether they reflect the character of a good person or organisation. Business ethics uses these lenses together to reach more balanced decisions (Crane et al., 2019).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

The theories go back to Bentham and Mill (consequentialism), Kant (deontology) and Aristotle (virtue). Andrew Crane, Dirk Matten and co-authors adapted them for managers in their widely used textbook Business Ethics (Crane et al., 2019).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Managers need practical ways to argue about right and wrong beyond 'it is legal' or 'it is profitable'. Combining several theories avoids blind spots of a single rule.

FIGURE 1 · THREE ETHICAL THEORIES

Figure 1. Judge an action by its outcomes, its duties and its character.

HOW IT IS USED

Describe the decision. Ask three questions: who gains and who is harmed (outcomes)? Which rights or duties are respected or broken (duties)? What would a fair and honest company do (character)? Note where the answers conflict.

WHEN IT IS USED

Before launching a product, signing a supplier contract or changing data practices.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Founders, product teams, ethics boards, journalists and students in debates.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Outsourcing data labelling to Nairobi may lower costs and create jobs (outcomes), but if workers lack adequate psychological support, duties of care are at stake (duties).

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Crane and Matten placed classic theories in the context of globalisation and corporate citizenship. They showed managers how to use theories pragmatically rather than choosing one 'correct' theory (Crane et al., 2019).

When designing a business model, a supply chain or a sustainability report.

Managers, CSR teams, founders and students analysing cases.

Identify direct and indirect stakeholders (including workers who never use the product). Name the values at stake and possible conflicts. Study stakeholders empirically, then make technical design choices that support those values.

WHEN IT IS USED

From idea and prototype stage onwards, before features and data flows are fixed.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Designers, product managers, engineers and founders of digital ventures.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Designing a content-moderation tool with value sensitive design would include the moderators as indirect stakeholders, e.g. blurring images by default and limiting daily exposure.

Surveillance Capitalism

How behavioural data turns into predictions and power

SLIBRARY 20 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Surveillance capitalism describes an economic logic in which companies collect data on human behaviour beyond what is needed to improve a service, turn this 'behavioural surplus' into predictions, and sell those predictions in markets for future behaviour. Zuboff argues this concentrates knowledge and power in a few firms (Zuboff, 2019).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Shoshana Zuboff, professor emerita at Harvard Business School, developed the concept in articles from 2015 and in her 2019 book (Zuboff, 2019).

Cross-Case Synthesis

Turning many separate case stories into tested, transferable patterns

SLIBRARY 21 · SHEET 1 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Cross-case synthesis is an analytic technique that compares two or more cases on a common set of dimensions, usually in a word table, to identify replicated patterns and explain differences. Findings are strengthened when a pattern holds across cases and survives deliberate comparison with contrasting cases (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 2018).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Kathleen M. Eisenhardt of Stanford University set out a roadmap for building theory from multiple case studies in the Academy of Management Review (Eisenhardt, 1989). Robert K. Yin described cross-case synthesis as a named analytic technique in successive editions of his case study textbook (Yin, 2018).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Single cases are vivid but easy to over-interpret. Researchers and managers needed a disciplined way to compare cases so that conclusions rest on repeated evidence rather than on one memorable story.

FIGURE 1 · CROSS-CASE SYNTHESIS

Figure 1. Same questions, many cases: read down the columns for patterns.

HOW IT IS USED

Choose cases and a fixed set of comparison questions, fill a case-by-question matrix, then look for patterns within columns and contrasts between successful and failed cases. Test each emerging pattern against counter-examples before stating it as a lesson.

WHEN IT IS USED

Used whenever a venture learns from several examples at once: competitor scans, post-mortems of failed products, or comparing pilot markets before scaling.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Used by researchers, strategy consultants, investors comparing portfolio companies and founders benchmarking rivals.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Comparing Vinted and Gorillas on unit economics and growth speed shows why a patient marketplace reached profit while a cash-hungry quick-commerce model was sold below its peak valuation.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Earlier case work tended to treat each case as a stand-alone narrative. Eisenhardt (1989) added replication logic and structured cross-case comparison, so that cases function like a series of experiments.

Pyramid Principle

Structure every message top-down: conclusion, key arguments, supporting evidence

SLIBRARY 21 · SHEET 2 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

The Pyramid Principle is a method for structuring business communication in which the main conclusion is stated first and supported by a small number of logically grouped key arguments, each backed by evidence. Ideas at each level summarise those grouped below them (Minto, 2009).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Barbara Minto, the first female MBA hired by McKinsey & Company in 1963, developed the approach while training consultants to write clearly; it was published as The Pyramid Principle (Minto, 2009).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Busy readers struggled with reports that walked through analysis chronologically before reaching the point. Minto wanted a structure that delivers the answer quickly and makes the logic easy to check.

FIGURE 1 · PYRAMID PRINCIPLE

Figure 1. Answer first, then group and support your arguments.

HOW IT IS USED

Write the governing thought (your answer) first, then the three or so key arguments that support it, then the data under each argument. Check that each group is mutually exclusive and that the summary truly follows from what sits beneath it.

WHEN IT IS USED

Used for pitch decks, investor updates, board memos and final project presentations — any time a decision-maker has little time.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Used by consultants, founders, analysts and students presenting recommendations.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A group presenting its Digital Venture Project opens with ‘Our app can reach break-even in 18 months’, then gives three reasons (demand evidence, unit economics, channel), each with one data point.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Traditional reports followed a bottom-up ‘background, analysis, conclusion’ order. Minto (2009) inverted this into a top-down logical hierarchy that matches how readers process and remember ideas.

Six building blocks for arguments that persuade and survive challenge

SLIBRARY 21 · SHEET 3 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

The Toulmin Model breaks a practical argument into six parts: claim, data (grounds), warrant, backing, qualifier and rebuttal. It shows how evidence is connected to a conclusion and how strong that conclusion really is (Toulmin, 1958).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

British philosopher Stephen Toulmin introduced the model in The Uses of Argument, published by Cambridge University Press (Toulmin, 1958).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Toulmin argued that formal logic did not describe how people actually argue in law, science or everyday decisions. He wanted a model of practical reasoning that includes uncertainty and exceptions.

FIGURE 1 · TOULMIN MODEL OF ARGUMENT

Figure 1. Claim, data, warrant — plus the honesty of qualifier and rebuttal.

HOW IT IS USED

State your claim, give the data that supports it, and spell out the warrant that links the two. Add backing for the warrant, a qualifier showing how certain you are, and the rebuttal conditions under which the claim would not hold.

WHEN IT IS USED

Used when a venture must defend a decision under scrutiny: investor questions, ethics reviews, roundtables and assessment defences.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Used by lawyers, debaters, policy analysts, founders and students writing evidence-based arguments.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

Claim: AI start-ups must verify revenue before scaling. Data: Builder.ai’s 2025 insolvency. Warrant: investors fund evidence. Qualifier: usually. Rebuttal: unless growth is cash-generative from day one.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Classical syllogisms assumed certain premises and certain conclusions. Toulmin (1958) added warrants, qualifiers and rebuttals, making room for probability and context in real-world reasoning.

Meetings often become arguments in which people defend positions rather than explore an issue. De Bono wanted a simple tool that lets everyone think in the same direction at the same time.

FIGURE 1 · SIX THINKING HATS

HOW IT IS USED

A facilitator (blue hat) sets the sequence; the group then spends a few minutes in each hat, for example white for facts, green for ideas, yellow for value, black for risks and red for gut feeling. The blue hat closes with a summary and next steps.

WHEN IT IS USED

Used in idea reviews, peer feedback, strategy workshops and roundtables where a team needs balanced input quickly.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Used by facilitators, product teams, teachers and start-up founders running team meetings.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

A Munich student team reviewing its delivery-app idea spends five minutes in the black hat listing risks Gorillas faced, then five in the green hat inventing ways to avoid them.

Traditional debate relies on argument and counter-argument between opposing sides. De Bono (1985) replaced this with parallel thinking, where all participants explore the same perspective together.

WHEN IT IS USED

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Used by team coaches, trainers, managers and student project teams.

Peer cards tell a founder he interrupts colleagues in meetings (blind area); he shares that he is anxious about the deadline (hidden area) and agrees to let others finish first.

A quick team debrief that turns every project into shared lessons

SLIBRARY 22 · SHEET 4 / 4
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

An After-Action Review (AAR) is a structured, non-blaming team discussion after an event that compares what was supposed to happen with what actually happened, explains the difference and agrees what to sustain or improve. It turns team experience into immediate, shared lessons (Garvin, 2000).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

The method was developed by the US Army for training exercises and later documented in its Leader’s Guide to After-Action Reviews (Department of the Army, 1993); Harvard professor David A. Garvin analysed and popularised it for business (Garvin, 2000).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Units needed to learn quickly between exercises without waiting for formal reports or assigning blame. A short, honest, shared debrief made lessons available to the whole team immediately.

FIGURE 1 · AFTER-ACTION REVIEW

Figure 1. Expected, happened, why, next time — in fifteen minutes.

HOW IT IS USED

Straight after an event, the team answers four questions: What did we intend? What actually happened? Why was there a difference? What will we keep and change next time? A facilitator keeps it factual and rank-free.

WHEN IT IS USED

Used after sprints, launches, pitches, campaigns and failed experiments — ideally within hours.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Used by military units, hospitals, firefighters, product teams and start-ups.

EXAMPLE FROM DIGITAL BUSINESS

After today’s rehearsal a team notes: intended 5 minutes, took 8; reason, too many market numbers; next time, one chart and the answer first.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Traditional post-mortems were often formal, delayed and focused on blame. The AAR made reviews quick, routine and owned by the team itself (Darling, Parry and Moore, 2005).
