BDIBM313 · Unit 9 · Pitch Simulation

Pitch Simulation — Runway or Runaway?

Teams build a burn-rate and runway sheet, pitch to a student VC panel and defend their numbers under due diligence.

Financing Well-informed Digital Entrepreneurial Ventures · Format: Pitch Simulation

Learning Focus · ILOs 3 & 4

Evaluate how founders reflect on and lead fundraising decisions, and communicate how digital ventures are financed differently from traditional businesses, using burn rate, runway and investor criteria.

Investors do not fund ideas; they fund evidence that money will turn into growth before it runs out.

Warm-up · 10 min · The €1 Million Envelope

Each student gets an envelope with ten paper tokens worth €100,000 each. In two minutes, they must decide how to spend €1 million in a start-up's first year across five labelled boxes on the front desk: people, product, marketing, servers and reserve. The lecturer counts the boxes aloud and asks the students who left the reserve box empty what happens in month 13.

The Provocation

Builder.ai raised more than 450 million dollars. In May 2025 it had about five million left.

A VC who says 'too early' often means 'not enough evidence'.

Your runway is not your plan. It is your deadline.

Revenue you cannot prove is not revenue. It is a story.

Read the lines with the Builder.ai timeline on screen. Then ask the room: 'Which number would you have checked first as an investor?' and keep the answers for the due-diligence step.

How the Activity Runs

1
Build the runway sheet25 min

Teams of four receive a one-page fact sheet for a fictional Munich digital start-up (cash, monthly costs, revenue, growth). On a printed A3 sheet they calculate: net monthly burn = monthly cash out − monthly cash in; runway (months) = cash ÷ net monthly burn; fume date = today + runway; latest fundraising start = fume date − time to clear (assume 6 months) − 2-month buffer. They also apply the default-alive test: does revenue growth reach break-even before the fume date?

2
Pitch build and panel briefing20 min

Each team prepares a five-slide, five-minute pitch adapted from Kawasaki's 10/20/30 rule: problem, solution and model, traction, runway and use of funds, the ask. The ask must say how much they raise, whether equity, a KfW-backed bank loan or venture debt, and how many months of runway it buys. Meanwhile one member per team rotates onto the VC panel and receives a due-diligence checklist based on Gompers et al. (2020) with a red-flag column for revenue that cannot be verified.

3
Pitch rounds with student VC panel45 min

Each team pitches for five minutes followed by five minutes of panel Q&A, with no laptops for the panel. Panellists must ask at least one question on burn, one on runway and one on evidence of revenue. Each panellist privately allocates virtual investment tokens and writes one reason for a 'no' — the most common real reason VCs turn start-ups down.

4
Funding decision and term sheet15 min

The panel announces which teams receive funding and hands a simple term sheet (amount, pre-money valuation, 1x liquidation preference, one board seat). Teams calculate their dilution on a mini cap table and decide in five minutes whether to accept, negotiate or choose debt or bootstrapping instead, justifying it with the pecking order.

5
Debrief: what money cannot buy10 min

The class compares the reasons for 'no' with the Builder.ai case and the VC-decision factors. Each student writes one sentence on what they would change in their own Digital Venture Project financial plan.

Deliverable. Each team hands in its completed A3 runway sheet (burn, runway, fume date, fundraising start date, default-alive verdict) and a photo of its five pitch slides with the panel's funding decision.
