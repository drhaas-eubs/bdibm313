BDIBM313 · Unit 8 · Pre-Reading

Pre-Reading: Impact Inside the Engine, Not on the Paintwork

Key framework: Triple-Layered Business Model Canvas

Sustainable Entrepreneurship · Format: World Café

Learning Focus · ILOs 3 & 4

Evaluate how digital ventures build social and environmental value into their business models and support networks, and communicate how this differs from traditional, add-on approaches to sustainability.

Key framework: Triple-Layered Business Model Canvas

The Triple-Layered Business Model Canvas adds an environmental layer (based on life-cycle thinking) and a social layer (based on stakeholders) underneath the familiar economic Business Model Canvas. It lets founders see whether impact is part of how the business creates value or merely a message on top.

Key Points

  • Sustainable entrepreneurs create economic, environmental and social value at the same time, not one after the other.
  • The triple bottom line asks founders to measure people, planet and profit — but Elkington himself warned it is often reduced to accounting.
  • The Triple-Layered Business Model Canvas makes trade-offs between the three layers visible on one page.
  • Digital tools enable impact, but people must accept them, and founders need moral and professional support to keep learning.

Read Before Class

In 1811 English textile workers known as Luddites smashed machines that threatened their jobs. Today 'luddite' is an insult for anyone who resists technology, yet historians point out that the Luddites were protesting against how machines were used, not against machines as such (Jones, 2006; Merchant, 2023). That distinction matters for sustainable founders. The perceptive entrepreneur neither rejects digital tools nor adopts them blindly: they ask who benefits, who pays, and what happens to the planet. Sustainable entrepreneurship is exactly this — creating economic value while also contributing to environmental and social progress (Schaltegger and Wagner, 2011).

Elkington (1997) gave this idea a popular name: the triple bottom line of people, planet and profit. Twenty-one years later he argued that many companies had reduced it to a reporting exercise, so the concept had failed to bury the single bottom line paradigm (Elkington, 2018). His warning is the core message of today's unit: impact that lives only in the sustainability report or the marketing campaign is 'bolted on'. Impact that sits inside the value proposition, the revenue streams and the partner network is 'built in', and it grows when the company grows.

Joyce and Paquin (2016) turned this into a practical tool. Their Triple-Layered Business Model Canvas keeps Osterwalder and Pigneur's nine economic blocks and adds two more layers. The environmental layer asks about functional value, materials, production, distribution, use phase, end-of-life, environmental impacts and benefits. The social layer asks about social value, employees, governance, communities, societal culture, scale of outreach, end-users, social impacts and benefits. Reading the layers horizontally shows coherence within one layer; reading them vertically shows how a decision in one layer affects the others.

Look at Too Good To Go before class. Its revenue (a fee per Surprise Bag) is directly linked to its environmental benefit (food saved) and its social value (affordable meals, extra income for shops). But no model is perfect: founders must keep checking unintended effects, persuade partners to adopt the app, and keep learning as technology and regulation change. Davis (1989) reminds us that people adopt tools they find useful and easy to use; Kram (1985) shows that mentors provide both career and psychosocial support. Bring one example of 'built-in' and one of 'bolted-on' impact to the café.
